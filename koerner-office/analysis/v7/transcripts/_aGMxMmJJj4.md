## Tactics and playbook steps

**Choose a narrow, reachable local-business niche** (niche_selection)
1. Look for a large pool of businesses and signs of current demand using search trends.
2. Prefer small operators whose decision-maker may be reachable directly.
3. Check Google Maps listings for a phone number, reviews, service area, and existing website.
4. Prioritise businesses with no site or a weak site, while recognising the host says prospects with an existing site may already understand its value.
> "The biggest reason of all is that of all the thousands of home service niches out there, handyman are probably one of the most likely to have the owner's personal cell phone number listed right there on Google Maps for the whole world to see." — Chris Koerner (~5:04)

**Generate a repeatable website prompt** (operations)
1. Ask a general-purpose AI model to write a detailed prompt for GoHighLevel's site builder.
2. Specify the business type and location.
3. Ask for CRM, calendar, workflows, SEO metadata, design, and local service-area coverage.
4. Use a reference website and list the target suburbs, then review the generated prompt before using it.
> "It tells High Level to integrate everything to the CRM, calendar, workflows, everything. SEO, metadescriptions, design, name your suburbs." — Chris Koerner (~2:52)

**Build a preview before pitching** (sales)
1. Create a site using the prospect's business name, location, and phone number.
2. Use a local reference site for inspiration, but tailor the content to the prospect.
3. Check that the site is built in the correct GoHighLevel tool; the host says he initially used the chatbot and then moved the HTML into AI Studio.
4. Connect the site to the CRM calendar and show the actual preview to the business owner.
> "Thankfully, Gohigh Level made it easy and I can just copy and paste my HTML into the Vibe coding tool." — Chris Koerner (~9:06)

**Personalise the outreach and show proof** (acquisition)
1. Find the owner or business name from the listing and check whether the displayed number is a mobile number.
2. Identify yourself by name and say you are local.
3. Send a screenshot of the work you actually built before asking permission to send the link.
4. Adapt the pitch if the business already has a website; the host tests wording about a weak site.
> "I'm going to send him a screenshot of what I actually did so he can know that I stand out." — Chris Koerner (~10:50)

**Test pitch variations** (sales)
1. Try a version for businesses with no site and a different version for businesses with an existing site.
2. Compare a message with a screenshot against one without it.
3. Use the city name or other genuine local detail to establish relevance.
4. Do not claim to have made a personalised video or other work if it has not actually been made.
> "I'm going to test not sending the screenshot because there's a chance that that screenshot is sending my message to spam and I want to avoid that." — Chris Koerner (~13:13)

**Anchor the monthly plan with a setup-fee option** (pricing)
1. Present a setup-fee plan with fewer included services.
2. Offer a monthly plan without setup fee that includes a custom domain and hosting.
3. Set the premium plan price separately; the host does not give its amount.
4. Treat the example prices as testable suggestions rather than validated market rates.
> "And then you're saying for only 75 a month you don't have any setup fee and you get a custom domain and I'll host it for you." — Chris Koerner (~16:29)

**Use a free sample as a trust-building offer** (offer_design)
1. Show a finished website rather than asking the owner to imagine one.
2. Offer the site free as an alternative model when earning trust is the priority.
3. Define what hosting and support cover before making a free-for-life promise.
4. Plan for a different upsell path because the host says free can be easier to accept but harder to upsell.
> "This website is the free sample. And but I do feel strongly that you need to charge for it." — Chris Koerner (~14:46)

**Use client websites as a route to add-on services** (sales)
1. Start with the website as the first paid service or trust-building offer.
2. Identify relevant add-ons such as voice answering, ads, content, booking, lead generation, customer service, or email marketing.
3. Sell only services that can be delivered reliably; the transcript does not provide proven conversion rates or delivery details.
> "But this is where the real real money will be made is on the upsell packages." — Chris Koerner (~17:30)

## Lessons

- *offer_design*: Show a working example so a prospect can judge the offer directly instead of having to imagine the result.
- *niche_selection*: Look for evidence that a niche has both demand and reachable decision-makers before choosing it.
- *operations*: Use a repeatable template and customise the business name, location, and relevant service areas for each customer.
- *offer_design*: A free offer can lower the barrier to a first yes, but the host says it can make later upsells harder.
- *pricing*: Use a setup-fee option to make the recurring plan easier to compare, and make the included services explicit.
- *scaling*: A low-priced first service can create a path to related services, but the host's upsell conversion estimate is not validated in the video.
- *validation_testing*: Test outreach details such as screenshots and wording rather than assuming one message will work.
- *customer_acquisition*: Be truthful about the work already done and use genuine local details to establish credibility.
- *legal_risk*: Check compliance before contacting prospects; manual sending does not remove legal requirements for marketing messages.
- *validation_testing*: A fast build is not proof of customer demand; measure actual replies and sales before relying on the revenue projections.
