## Tactics and playbook steps

**Choose a product suited to direct-to-consumer manufacturing** (niche_selection)
1. Look for a product you can manufacture competitively in your home market.
2. Consider how product size and shipping costs affect the feasibility of importing it.
3. Use direct sales to the end customer to remove intermediaries.
> "I wanted to own a product that I could manufacture competitively in the US and sell direct to consumer in the US." — David Heacock (~1:50)

**Use daily operational dashboards and intervene early** (operations)
1. Build dashboards that show the health of each part of the operation.
2. Check them every morning and identify overloaded locations, stock problems and risks to delivery promises.
3. Shift eligible orders to centers that can still meet promised dates.
4. Add a delivery buffer when a center risks missing its promise.
> "I was waking up at 5:30 in the morning. I was looking at the dashboards that we had built letting me know what's happening in any part of the business." — David Heacock (~9:25)

**Rebalance production and inventory across locations** (operations)
1. Identify which centers are exceeding their production capacity and which have spare capacity.
2. Make additional product at centers with room to produce it.
3. Ship that product to the overloaded region.
4. Fix order routing so orders can move to locations with more capacity.
> "We had a few centers, particularly in the Northeast, that got absolutely slammed. And when that happened, they were selling more than their production capabilities." — David Heacock (~23:04)

**Treat inventory counts as an operating process** (operations)
1. Check that people follow the inventory-counting process consistently.
2. Investigate discrepancies quickly instead of treating them as a software-only problem.
3. Correct the counts and combine that work with routing and replenishment changes.
> "Ultimately, the root cause here was a breakdown in our inventory system. Not from a software perspective, but from like the system of the people that are responsible for taking part in each of it." — David Heacock (~19:27)

**Learn critical jobs and create a daily promise dashboard** (hiring)
1. Write down every job in the business.
2. Mark the jobs you personally could not cover if the employee quit tomorrow.
3. Learn those jobs well enough to act as a fail-safe.
4. Build one dashboard around the key customer promise and check it every morning.
> "Number one, write down every job in your business and mark the ones you personally could not do tomorrow if that person quit overnight." — David Heacock (~26:17)

**Check delivery capacity before increasing demand** (operations)
1. Before spending more on ads or accepting more customers, assess whether the team can serve the orders already sold.
2. Fix service capacity and delivery reliability before pursuing more growth.
> "Before you spend more on ads or take on more customers, be straight with yourself about whether you can service what you've already sold." — David Heacock (~26:52)

**Visit newer locations to reinforce culture** (operations)
1. Identify newer locations, teams and hires where culture and process knowledge may be less established.
2. Spend time there in person, observe the work and discuss why the processes matter.
> "If you've got a location, a team, or a hire that's newer than the rest, go spend time there in person" — David Heacock (~27:06)

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Choose a business where the product and market give you a structural advantage, such as manufacturing locally when shipping makes imports less practical.
- *operations*: Growth only helps when the business can keep the promises it makes to customers.
- *scaling*: Unexpected demand can reveal weaknesses in staffing, inventory, routing and management processes.
- *operations*: Use operational metrics to spot problems early, then make specific changes such as rerouting orders or buffering delivery promises.
- *validation_testing*: When a serious operational failure happens, look for root causes and process gaps rather than assuming the software is at fault.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: A founder can delegate more effectively after learning the work, documenting processes and building systems that can be handed to a capable steward.
- *operations*: Newer teams and locations need direct attention because dashboards cannot show whether culture and process understanding are taking hold.
- *mindset_execution*: Treat business momentum as something to protect, while still fixing the operational issues that could undermine it.
- *automation_ai*: Build inventory, routing and delivery systems by testing them under pressure, then making them more robust.
- *operations*: Use the CEO role to support employees, understand root problems and remove blockers instead of simply taking over the work.

## Warnings and mistakes

- More sales can create operational problems if the business cannot deliver what it promised.
- Keeping very little inventory leaves little margin for forecasting or counting errors.
- A robust software system can still fail when people do not follow the operating process.
- A key employee can resign without notice, so the business should not depend on one person being the only fail-safe.
- Delegating before learning how the work and systems function can prevent a founder from building a robust, scalable business.
- Culture can weaken as a company adds locations if leaders do not spend time aligning people on the work and its purpose.
