## Tactics and playbook steps

**Find offices already using jug delivery** (acquisition)
1. Look for offices still stacking bottled-water jugs.
2. Walk in and offer to remove the jug-handling and storage problem with a bottleless cooler.
3. Start with building managers and office managers the operator already knows.
4. Use a first placement in a building to approach other floors.
> "Walk into an office still stacking jugs and offer to make the jugs disappear." — Harry (~3:10)

**Price water service for usage and value** (pricing)
1. Estimate filter use based on the number of people served.
2. Put filter changes on a calendar.
3. Price the service visit before installing the machine.
4. Sell water quality and reliability instead of discounting the monthly fee.
5. Use longer agreements for new customers when the customer accepts them.
> "So, he repriced by headcount, the way the industry already does it, put filter changes on a calendar, and moved new hosts to three-year agreements." — Harry (~11:29)

**Build a local diffuser refill route** (operations)
1. Place a first unit in a salon or waiting room.
2. Refill it monthly.
3. Ask nearby businesses for introductions and add hosts next door.
4. Group visits into a route that can be completed in one afternoon.
> "Start with one salon or one waiting room, refill it once a month, and add the business next door." — Harry (~5:56)

**Sell AED readiness instead of a device** (offer_design)
1. Check the local rules before offering a program.
2. Find a medical director to provide the stated physician oversight.
3. Approach gyms the operator already knows.
4. Offer readiness checks and logging, with the device and CPR training as package options.
> "Then you walk into the gyms you already know and offer the readiness plan, not the box." — Harry (~8:08)

**Use a struggling ice machine as the sales trigger** (acquisition)
1. Ask restaurant supply stores and refrigeration technicians for leads.
2. Listen for bar owners discussing a machine that is failing.
3. Offer a rental that includes installation, cleaning, and repairs.
4. Hire qualified refrigeration and plumbing contractors for installation.
5. Clean the machine every six months to protect its useful life.
> "Then find one bar whose own machine is limping along. That's your first customer." — Harry (~10:42)

**Cross-sell dishwashers and chemicals to restaurant accounts** (sales)
1. Start with restaurants already buying another machine service from the operator.
2. Offer the dishwasher and scheduled chemical supply as a combined service.
3. Use a written agreement and schedule chemical deliveries.
> "So, the second invoice lands at a door they already walk through." — Harry (~13:44)

**Cross-sell coffee to cooler customers** (sales)
1. Start with offices already renting a water cooler.
2. Offer coffee service to the same office manager and building.
3. Place a simple brewer in suitable offices and deliver beans every two weeks.
4. Price per head so invoices grow when the office hires more people.
5. Upgrade the best accounts to more capable machines.
> "Start with the offices that already rent your water cooler. Same building, same office manager, same monthly invoice." — Harry (~16:04)

**Inspect existing equipment for a service-company lead** (validation_testing)
1. When visiting an office, gym, or restaurant, find the service sticker on its water cooler or ice machine.
2. Write down the service company's name and phone number.
3. Treat an owner-operated machine as a possible host without an existing service contract.
> "The next time you walk into an office, a gym, or a restaurant, look for the sticker on the water cooler or the ice machine." — Harry (~17:46)

## Lessons

- *offer_design*: Sell the outcome the customer wants, such as cold water or ice during service, rather than focusing on the machine itself.
- *offer_design*: A recurring fee works when it removes an ongoing task or failure risk the customer does not want to manage.
- *pricing*: Sell on reliability and value instead of entering a race to the lowest price.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Price the service burden before placing equipment, taking customer size and consumable usage into account.
- *customer_acquisition*: Use the first customer in a building or existing account to create a path to adjacent customers and add-on services.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Recurring consumable orders can make equipment placement viable, even when the equipment itself is placed for free.
- *operations*: Maintenance is part of the product and affects how long an asset keeps earning.
- *niche_selection*: Look for businesses where the customer values uptime and wants to avoid owning a maintenance problem.
- *operations*: Use regular service intervals and recurring invoices to make a route and its workload predictable.
- *validation_testing*: Before investing in equipment, check whether the host has a maintenance problem, whether a second service can be sold at the same site, and whether fees repay the asset within the agreement.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Check local rules before installing or servicing water coolers; the transcript explicitly advises checking city rules.
- Do not win water cooler deals only by promising the lowest price; the video says discounting trapped Quench salespeople at barely profitable prices.
- Do not charge customers the same price when their filter consumption and service burden differ substantially.
- AED programs involve expiring pads and batteries, readiness records, and physician oversight; check applicable rules and ensure the equipment remains ready.
- The video says AED rules differ by state and advises checking the rules before starting.
- Ice machines require competent plumbing and refrigeration installation, and health inspectors check them; check local rules.
- Poor maintenance can cut an ice machine's life to four to six years; regular cleaning is part of the economics.
- Dishwasher installations require a plumber and electrician, and chemical deliveries need to stay on schedule.
- Equipment businesses require physical visits for installation, maintenance, restocking, or repair, which limits how independently agents can run delivery.
