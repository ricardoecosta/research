## Tactics and playbook steps

**Apply with an existing social account, then build engagement if rejected** (validation_testing)
1. Try a public account you already have, such as Facebook, YouTube, Pinterest, or TikTok.
2. If rejected, choose a niche for a new TikTok account.
3. Follow a few niche accounts, like and comment on related posts, then post niche content consistently for about two weeks.
4. Use questions to invite comments; Nate also suggests rage bait, which may harm trust and should be used cautiously.
> "If you've got a public Facebook page, you've got a big YouTube, you've got even a small YouTube." — Nate (~2:59)

**Start with products already at hand** (operations)
1. Walk through your home and identify products, household hardware, furniture, books, clothing, tools, and used items that match Amazon listings.
2. Film an item you already own instead of spending the same time researching whether it is a perfect opportunity.
3. Use a visual search tool to match an item to a listing before filming; Nate says not to film first and match later.
> "I could spend that same five minutes simply making a video for this mouse and uploading it and doing zero research." — Nate (~7:48)

**Keep simple reviews quick and useful** (operations)
1. Use a phone or camera and talk through what the product is, what you like, what you dislike, and who it suits.
2. Show the item in use when practical; use a face-on-camera format when it helps, but film the product alone when that is clearer.
3. Skip editing out minor mistakes or the start button when the edit adds no useful information.
4. Use ChatGPT for talking points if a beginner needs help structuring a review.
> "I just point my camera, I hold the product like with this mouse, I keep the mouse close to my face." — Nate (~10:21)

**Test length and format against the likely shopper** (product)
1. Nate's current videos average two to three minutes; he previously aimed for roughly 45 to 85 seconds.
2. Test making a video 30% to 50% longer to increase watch time, while recognizing that Amazon's placement algorithm changes.
3. Use vertical video when shoppers are more likely to be on phones, such as for fashion or children's products; use horizontal for desktop-oriented products.
4. Try additional formats on the same product, such as an unboxing, an update, or a second orientation.
> "So for me, that's been make my videos maybe 30 to 50% longer than I normally would, and that seemed to be working well for me." — Nate (~13:49)

**Make multiple useful angles for strong products** (product)
1. For a product that merits more coverage, make separate videos for unboxing, setup, features, mistakes, comparison, and a later update.
2. Use measurements or real-life demonstrations when they answer a likely shopper question.
3. Keep the review honest, including problems with a product and who should avoid it.
> "So you can kind of come at it from so many different angles. There's not like a one size fits all for these things." — Nate (~21:43)

**Use nearby households and real environments as product sources** (acquisition)
1. Ask neighbors or friends for permission to film products they already own.
2. Nate offered a $20 Starbucks gift card for an afternoon of access to a large Lego collection.
3. Offer to house-sit or return for another session if a household has more products than can be filmed in one visit.
4. Film products in their natural environment, such as a handwashing station at a market, but do not film products inside retail stores.
> "I went to a couple of these houses. And I mean, I kid you not. These people have entire basements." — Nate (~45:24)

**Prioritize products with campaign commissions and manageable competition** (product)
1. Scan possible products and check influencer counts and Creator Connections campaign information.
2. When time is limited, prioritize products with a strong campaign rate and fewer competing influencers.
3. Treat selection filters such as 500 monthly units, zero influencers, or a 20% campaign rate as examples to test, not guaranteed predictors.
> "I need to sort of prioritize stuff. So I'll use that to do some visual scans." — Nate (~39:05)

**Ask brands for samples, but account for the work and competition** (acquisition)
1. Find relevant Creator Connections campaigns and send a clear request to the brand for a product to review.
2. Do not assume the campaign statement about samples means the brand will refuse; Nate says it may be boilerplate.
3. Track response rates and product handling time, and do not let free samples crowd out simpler products already available.
4. In the UK, screen the recipient type and ensure any electronic marketing outreach complies with PECR.
> "So request anything you want, and about 10% are going to reach back to you and say, OK, great." — Nate (~43:53)

**Cross-post completed videos automatically** (operations)
1. Set up Chiching to download Amazon videos, add descriptions and titles, change thumbnails, add affiliate links, and upload them to YouTube.
2. Let the tool run in the background, then check that uploads and links are correct.
3. Treat YouTube as an incremental revenue stream unless you are intentionally producing content for YouTube viewers.
> "So I let this run and it usually runs like 10 to 20 videos per day 'cause I have a lot of videos" — Nate (~30:10)

**Try low-cost returns stock and resell products that do not perform** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Look for local return-bin or liquidation outlets and scan products before buying.
2. Compare product cost with the expected resale price, campaign terms, and marketplace fees.
3. List unwanted or reviewed products for resale through marketplaces; consider live selling only if enough inventory accumulates.

**Build a second income stream as a safety net** (finance)
1. Nate says he is building a YouTube channel and coaching activity so he has some income if Amazon shuts down his account.
2. Keep secondary revenue sources as a baseline rather than assuming they will replace the main business.
> "I've been doing some coaching with people down just helping to teach other people how to do this." — Nate (~61:56)

## Lessons

- *mindset_execution*: Publish a simple first version instead of waiting for ideal research, equipment, titles, or editing; practice improves the process.
- *validation_testing*: When products are already available to film, the opportunity cost of researching every item may exceed the cost of trying a quick video.
- *finance_unit_economics*: A high-volume approach spreads effort across many videos, but it also means accepting that many videos may earn nothing.
- *offer_design*: Use the product listing as context: shoppers are already considering a purchase, so a useful confirmation can matter more than a hard sell.
- *offer_design*: Honest negative feedback can build trust and still help a shopper decide; explain the real drawback and any appropriate alternative use.
- *validation_testing*: Prefer incremental experiments over trying to solve an algorithm that changes and exposes only partial performance data.
- *offer_design*: For physical product videos, show the item in a real setting when that setting helps shoppers understand how it works.
- *operations*: Treat free samples as a sourcing option, not automatically as the best growth strategy; competition and handling time can erase the appeal.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Build a second revenue stream to reduce reliance on one platform and create a financial safety net.
- *scaling*: Longer-lived content can keep earning after the production work is done, but past earnings do not remove the need to keep creating if income is declining.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Do not count on extreme income forecasts without considering account risk, changing algorithms, decay, and the labor needed to keep producing.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Do not overinvest in product research or production before publishing; Nate says beginners often get stuck in analysis paralysis.
- Amazon carousel placement is partly random and the algorithm changes, so do not assume one video length or title tactic will always work.
- Nate says not to film products on retail shelves in stores such as Walmart or Target because Amazon may remove an account.
- Free samples can create crowding, slower production, and packaging disposal work; a product that looks attractive in shared data may have many competing creators.
- Nate says on-site related-sale credit was removed in April, so negative reviews no longer earn related sales in the same way.
- Do not assume every video will earn; Nate describes many videos earning nothing and a small number earning more.
- Automated brand outreach should be checked against UK marketing rules, especially for messages to individuals and sole traders.
- Nate says not to expect a large income for doing no work; the business still requires filming, product handling, and continued effort.
