## Summary

David Heacock interviews JT, a former software engineer from Ohio who built JC Print Farm, a 3D printing business that grew from a few basement printers to about 105 machines and five staff. Revenue was $50k in year one, $400k in year two and $400k year-to-date in 2026; about half comes from their own product catalogue (gaming grips, sculptures, custom busts) sold on Amazon, Etsy, eBay and Shopify, with B2B short runs and print-on-demand fulfilment making up the rest. JT targets ~50% margins on own products versus ~10% on print-on-demand. David's advice is to stop drifting toward low-margin fulfilment and instead systematise a creative pipeline of niche product designs (more shots on goal) seeded to TikTok Shop micro-influencers on commission. Also covers co-founder buyout agreements, S-corp salary/distributions and saving for optionality.

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Etsy-first listing grind to find product-market fit** (product)
1. Start on Etsy, which enforces listing rules and runs Google ads for you
2. Publish many cheap experimental listings (they did ~30 before the first sale)
3. Let the algorithm show which category sells
4. Expand the catalogue around the winner (100+ grip variants)
5. Then add Amazon FBA, eBay and Shopify
> "Just keep grinding. You'll figure out what you're better at than everybody else. the algorithm will tell you when you start getting sales in that category." — JT (~10:18)

**Be the reason for your print farm** (product)
1. Don't buy printers and offer to 'print anything'
2. First create products that sell
3. Add capacity as your own demand grows
> "when I ask advise people how to start this is don't ask for people to print. Be the reason for your print farm." — JT (~67:02)

**TikTok Shop micro-influencer seeding** (acquisition)
1. Pick passionate niches (gaming, pets, gardening)
2. Design products with the influencer customer in mind
3. Find ~100 creators in the niche and send free product
4. Pay commission on TikTok Shop sales or pay for videos
5. Run ads on winners inside TikTok
6. List the same items on Amazon and elsewhere for spillover
> "find you know 100 influencers in that community. See them with the product, pay them a commission for um everything that they sell through Tik Tok shops." — David Heacock (~35:31)

**Systematised design pipeline (shots on goal)** (product)
1. Define target niches
2. Hire a designer (Upwork or nearshore Latin America) to produce a set number of designs a month, e.g. 10/month = 120/year
3. Grade ideas on virality and niche passion
4. List everywhere; expect a bell curve with a few outliers
5. Prefer functional, evergreen products, or niches too small for injection moulding
> "if you had 10 designs a month in the gaming niche that were just doing that and you're like, some of them are going to be duds" — David Heacock (~65:45)

**AI listing photos without printing every SKU** (operations)
1. Keep enough finished samples
2. Ask an AI image tool to render a finished listing photo from the digital design
3. Only print when an order arrives
> "Hey AI, this is what it looks like completely finished. Can you make me a listing photo of this?" — JT (~15:15)

**Keep printer utilisation high with FBA** (operations)
1. When order capacity drops, print stock for Amazon FBA
2. Accept lower margins to keep machine efficiency at 80-90%

**Give them the pickle: free surprise in every box** (product)
1. Add a small free branded item (a 3D-printed articulated frog) to every package
2. Encourages review photos and brand recognition
> "we have this little 3D printed articulated frog we give out." — JT (~72:43)

**Micro store plus Meta ads to double price** (pricing)
1. Build a small Shopify store for one product sector
2. Invest in site performance
3. Run Meta ads to the exact target customer
4. Price at roughly double marketplace prices
> "you can actually take your products, double the price and bring your own customers in and they'll still sell just as well." — JT (~55:38)

**Pre-agreed co-founder exit valuation** (legal)
1. Write a contract on how the company is valued if a partner leaves (e.g. formula under $1M)
2. Set a payoff schedule (they had three years)
3. Avoid giving equity to an advisor if possible
> "it said that like if we're under a million dollars, here's exactly how you value the company." — JT (~41:33)

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Validate demand with many cheap marketplace listings before investing in ads or your own site; the 31st listing was the first to sell.
- *offer_design*: Own-IP products give control over price and quality and are harder to copy than B2B or print-on-demand work.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Compare segments by margin and effort, not just volume: ~10% print-on-demand needs five times the work for the same profit.
- *niche_selection*: In 3D printing, win in niches too small for injection moulding or in hyper-personalised items, since filament makes custom units cost the same.
- *customer_acquisition*: Scale content without being on camera by paying creators commission; one organic creator video gave the best sales day.
- *scaling*: Build a repeatable creative system rather than hoping for one viral product.
- *pricing*: Bringing your own targeted traffic lets you charge more than on a crowded marketplace.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Savings and side income (rentals) give the optionality to survive a low first year.
- *operations*: A business's value lies in repeatable systems and processes, but creative and distribution systems matter as much as operations.
- *customer_acquisition*: Name products distinctly so people who hear about them on social media can search and find you.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Starting a print farm by buying printers and offering to print anything for others doesn't work.
- Mass-appeal products will be injection moulded and you can't compete; their Switch 2 grip flopped.
- Accessory products for devices expire when the next hardware generation launches; one hit listing fell from 95% of sales to under a quarter percent.
- Print-on-demand clients internalise production once volume grows, capping margins.
- Co-founders often diverge; without a pre-agreed exit contract, splits are painful.
- Paused Google ads because attribution wasn't set up; host says poor tracking is fixable and shouldn't stop a channel.
- Founders who find something that works often get distracted chasing other opportunities instead of doubling down.
