## Tactics and playbook steps

**Use an existing audience for a product launch** (acquisition)
1. Send a short email sequence to the existing list.
2. Use one email to tease the launch and another to demonstrate the software.
3. Open the cart after the launch emails.
4. For Link Whisper, the narrator says he pushed the product to his audience, but does not specify the exact channel.
> "I sent out the three emails sort of teasing the launch, demoing the software, and then I opened the cart" — Unidentified Niche Pursuits narrator (~2:06)

**Listen to customers and iterate on the offer** (product)
1. After a weak launch, continue testing rather than assuming the initial result is final.
2. Listen to customers and take customer calls.
3. Change features, pricing cadence and packages to look for a price and offer that customers will accept.
> "I was tweaking the features. I was listening to customers. I was doing calls with customers, and I was changing prices." — Unidentified Niche Pursuits narrator (~2:39)

**Check whether the market is large enough and willing to pay** (validation)
1. Estimate the number of plausible users before committing to a large build.
2. Check whether the target users already solve the problem with free tools.
3. Distinguish total users from the smaller group willing to pay for the offer.
> "I had overestimated the market size. I had thought everyone that it was an Amazon associate would want Table Labs." — Unidentified Niche Pursuits narrator (~3:11)

**Use a low-cost MVP to test a software idea** (operations)
1. Hire a developer for a few thousand dollars to get an MVP out the door.
2. Use an inexpensive logo rather than spending close to $1,000 on branding.
3. Buy a $20 WordPress theme instead of commissioning a custom website and theme.
4. Build the core product first, then reinvest after the product shows traction.
> "I went ahead and I hired an overseas-based developer for a few thousand dollars to get an MVP out the door." — Unidentified Niche Pursuits narrator (~4:59)

**Use an adjacent product's installations as a market signal** (validation)
1. Look at an established product used by the intended audience.
2. The narrator checked Yoast SEO's active installations on WordPress.org as an indication of interest in SEO on WordPress.
3. Treat this as a rough market-size signal, not proof that those users need or will buy the new product.
> "In fact, I went to WordPress.org and I looked at Yoast SEO to see how many free downloads their plugin has, because that's people that are interested in SEO and they're on WordPress." — Unidentified Niche Pursuits narrator (~5:55)

**Start with a low launch price** (pricing)
1. Set a low initial price to reduce the barrier to trying the software.
2. Use buyer response at that price to learn whether the market wants the product.
3. The narrator expected that buyers might later accept a higher price; he does not say that a later increase happened.
> "my launch pricing was just $47 per year. I came in low and I knew if I got buyers at that price, they might be willing to sustain a higher price after that." — Unidentified Niche Pursuits narrator (~6:22)

**Build the simple feature customers want** (product)
1. Identify the core customer need before building a large feature set.
2. Build a simple version of that feature and test it.
3. Avoid spending heavily on a logo and website before demand is clearer.
> "Figure out what the core customer wants and build that simple feature." — Unidentified Niche Pursuits narrator (~7:42)

## Lessons

- *finance_unit_economics*: Having capital does not guarantee business success; it can encourage a founder to overspend before validating demand.
- *niche_selection*: Estimate the paying market, not just the number of people who might encounter the problem.
- *validation_testing*: Account for free alternatives when testing whether customers will pay for a more polished or convenient product.
- *validation_testing*: Use customer conversations and changes to features and prices to learn when an offer is not gaining traction.
- *finance_unit_economics*: A low-cost MVP can test a software idea while limiting the amount at risk.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Keep early branding and web costs modest until there is evidence that the product is wanted.
- *offer_design*: Start with the core customer need and a simple feature rather than building a large product immediately.
- *pricing*: The narrator says a lower initial price helped Link Whisper launch, but his expectation of raising prices later is not evidence that a later increase worked.

## Warnings and mistakes

- A large audience or a broad apparent user base does not guarantee that enough people will pay. The narrator says he wrongly assumed every Amazon Associate would want Table Labs.
- Free alternatives can shrink the paying market even when a paid product is more polished or convenient.
- Do not assume customers will accept high prices just because the software works well; the narrator says his Table Labs prices were too high.
- Spending heavily on development, branding and a custom site before validating demand can expose a founder to a large loss.
- An installation count for an adjacent product is only a proxy; the narrator's estimate does not establish the number of customers for Link Whisper.
