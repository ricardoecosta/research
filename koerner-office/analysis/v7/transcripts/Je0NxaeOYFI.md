## Tactics and playbook steps

**Screen niches with customer and demand evidence** (validation_testing)
1. Start with specialist products that can retail for around $1,000 or more.
2. Look for a customer with a passion who could buy more than one related product.
3. Check search demand for product types and brands.
4. Check Google Shopping results for online-only competitors: enough to show online demand, but, in Phil's US example, not more than about 20 direct competitors.
5. Prefer suppliers based in the target market and products that are available for local delivery.
6. Look for around 10 or more potential suppliers so the business is not dependent on only a few.
> "So, I started researching those products. I'm also looking at things like search volumes. So, is there a good amount of search volume for those products" — Phil (~4:44)

**Secure supplier approval before listing a full catalogue** (sales)
1. Build a credible starter website with a designed homepage, some content and demo products before approaching suppliers.
2. Create a list of possible suppliers and look for their dealer or authorized retailer application pages.
3. Apply through the dealer page, or call and ask for a sales manager.
4. Explain the store, the value it can bring and how it will follow the supplier's brand guidelines.
5. Start with the first supplier approval; Phil says a single yes can be enough to begin.
> "You don't want to be ringing them up as someone that's not even got a website live that's got nothing. They're going to see you probably as a bit of a chance or so." — Phil (~12:32)

**Launch a small assortment and test Shopping Ads** (acquisition)
1. List the first approved products; Phil says one product can be enough to start, though he launched with products from four brands.
2. Use Google Shopping Ads to send high-intent searches directly to product pages.
3. Separate products into upper-, middle- and bottom-funnel campaigns.
4. Use negative keyword lists to exclude broad terms from lower-funnel campaigns and push more specific searches toward the campaign where bids can be higher.
5. Avoid relying on Performance Max at the beginning; Phil says he prefers a manual setup for high-ticket products.
> "I've never used it. I've never seen anything that says it works better than a manual approach for high ticket." — Phil (~21:13)

**Answer early customer questions by phone** (sales)
1. Put a phone number on the site so a customer can ask about a high-value product.
2. Listen to the customer's needs and check stock, delivery or specification questions with the supplier when needed.
3. Call the customer back with the answer and make it easy to complete payment online.
4. Use early calls to learn which questions customers ask and which products need better information.
> "If it's a question you don't have the answer to, you can say, I'm just going to check that with so-and-so all my manager and I'm going to give you a call back" — Phil (~28:13)

**Build SEO through specific questions and comparisons** (acquisition)
1. Use Answer the Public and Google autocomplete, People Also Ask and related searches to find real questions and longer search terms.
2. Target low-competition informational searches and specific product comparisons before broad category terms.
3. Create helpful product comparison posts and detailed collection pages that link to relevant products.
4. Build a repeatable SOP; Phil says he outsourced articles to a Fiverr freelancer and layered AI tools with a human edit for readability and accuracy.
> "Go to that, type to in the product category that I want to start building content around. So if we take saunas in this case, you go and search sauna and answer the public" — Phil (~42:55)

**Differentiate the offer with clear returns terms** (offer_design)
1. Make the returns policy easy to find and understand.
2. Consider whether a longer returns window or paying return postage could make the offer more attractive.
3. Check the supplier's own return window and condition requirements before promising a longer policy.
4. For eligible returns, send goods back to the supplier; Phil later used a 3PL for returned stock.
> "So returns policies, making sure that you've got a good, clear, visible returns policy, can you even make that the best returns policy in your market?" — Phil (~35:06)

**Leave employment only after sustained performance** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Build a savings runway from the business.
2. Look for a stable and growing revenue run over several consecutive months rather than relying on one unusually strong month.
3. Phil's personal threshold was 40,000 monthly revenue for six months in a row.
> "I wanted to see a stable and growing level over six months. For me, that was hitting 40,000 in revenue a month for six months in a row." — Phil (~47:19)

**Free founder time by outsourcing routine post-sale work** (operations)
1. Identify repetitive customer-service work such as tracking and order-status requests.
2. Outsource those routine tasks to a virtual assistant or team member.
3. Keep oversight of ads and larger sales projects, and reserve founder attention for decisions or significant customer issues.
> "The big time sap previously was customer service. It was fulfilling orders. It was where's my order, where's my tracking link, answering that type of stuff." — Phil (~48:17)

**Move from dropshipping to stock only after seeing repeat sales** (operations)
1. Start with dropshipping to avoid holding stock while demand is uncertain.
2. Track which SKUs sell consistently.
3. Consider buying bulk stock of proven products for better margins and more reliable availability.
4. Treat holding stock as a step toward the next stage, not as proof that every product should be stocked.
> "So we've built up that sales day to where we can confidently say, these are the handful of products that we know we're going to sell." — Phil (~49:16)

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Choose products with enough value per sale to leave room for advertising costs, support and delivery problems.
- *validation_testing*: Use evidence about customers, search demand, competition and suppliers to choose a niche; the founder does not have to be a customer personally.
- *validation_testing*: Existing demand can make an authorized-reseller offer easier to test than inventing a product before knowing whether customers want it.
- *mindset_execution*: A credible basic website and direct supplier conversations can be enough to begin; a large catalogue is not required to test the first product.
- *customer_acquisition*: High-ticket purchases often involve questions, so being available and useful before the sale can help distinguish a retailer from comparison-shopping competitors.
- *customer_acquisition*: For a young site, target specific, lower-competition searches before broad category terms, then build authority over time.
- *operations*: Outsource recurring support tasks that consume founder time, while keeping the founder focused on higher-value work and decisions.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Dropshipping is a lower-risk way to start and test demand, but holding stock of proven products can improve margins and availability.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Wait for a stable run of revenue and adequate savings before leaving a job; a single strong month may be a blip.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Revenue can hide weak economics. Track profit after advertising and other costs.
- *exit_acquisition*: Build a business with resale value in mind if that matters to the founder, but treat valuation multiples as uncertain rather than guaranteed.
