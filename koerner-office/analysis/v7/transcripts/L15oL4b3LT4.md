## Summary

Chris Koerner visits Golf Ranch in Richardson, Dallas, whose founding team (ex-Topgolf and long-time paid-ads operator) buys dated or abandoned driving ranges and modernises them. They upgraded ball and mat quality based on customer surveys, added covered bays, lights to midnight, music, string lights and a putting green, and replaced prepaid bucket cards with a $20/month Ranch Pass membership (40% off buckets); they now have 8,000 members across four sites and claim an LTV:CAC of about 10. Volume at this site went from about 3.5 million to 12 million balls a year. The key takeaway is that an old, underserved industry with a low bar can be won by listening to customers, a membership loop, loss-leader amenities and modern digital marketing; the goal is 50 sites and 100,000 members.

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Survey customers, then fix the top complaints first** (product)
1. Run customer surveys to get tangible data on where to start
2. Invest first in the #1 complaint (ball quality: sort balls, replace 120k/year)
3. Then #2 (turf/mats: buy every mat on Amazon, test, source from small distributors)
4. Call your best customers personally rather than relying on email surveys
> "Golf walls was the number one thing that we we got back in surveys. Number two was turf quality" — Guest (~1:48)

**Swap prepaid cards for a cancel-anytime membership** (pricing)
1. Replace prepaid bucket cards with a $20/month membership
2. Give 40% off buckets plus member events, par-three and lesson pricing
3. Make it cancel anytime to suit seasonal golfers
4. Use the commitment to layer programming (member competitions) that drives repeat visits
> "instead of prepaying for 10 buckets with one card, let's do a monthly membership model" — Guest (~3:35)

**Engineer the first and last five minutes** (operations)
1. Make arrival memorable
2. Do not rent time by the hour; let people stay as long as they want so they leave satisfied
3. Avoid a surprise bill at exit
> "the first five minutes of your visit and the last 5 minutes. That's what you remember" — Guest (~8:00)

**Loss-leader amenities (the Costco hot dog)** (pricing)
1. Build a putting green that earns nothing but draws serious golfers from 40 minutes away
2. Sell cheap coffee and simple food
3. Treat golf instruction as a loss leader and use instructors as salespeople for membership
> "This is my $150 hot dog loss leader. That is like a glue to attract real golfers" — Guest (~21:22)

**Extend revenue hours and weather-proof** (operations)
1. Add covers to bays for heat, rain and hail
2. Add fans and heaters
3. Add lights to stay open until midnight in summer
4. Keep a ball inventory backlog for days the outfield cannot be picked

**Spend capex only where the customer perceives it** (finance)
1. Keep the first ~30 yards of the outfield green; leave the rest
2. Use shipping containers and fresh paint instead of full buildouts
> "we made a decision like no, first 30 yards matters. After that, like doesn't really matter" — Guest (~25:23)

**Site selection for expansion** (acquisition)
1. Prefer existing ranges people already like that need TLC over raw land
2. Check public golf course count and whether they have practice facilities
3. Check demographics, favouring families

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Overlooked, dated industries have a low bar: modest upgrades beat incumbents.
- *validation_testing*: Talk to your customers; survey everyone and phone your best customers.
- *pricing*: Membership converts sporadic visits into habit and unlocks events and upsells.
- *niche_selection*: Identity-driven hobbies (golf, pickleball) create loyal, low-churn customers.
- *scaling*: Retention beats cheap acquisition: Topgolf's funnel is great but repeat visits are weak.
- *exit_acquisition*: Selling tech into old owner-operators is hard; buying the businesses and installing it yourself can capture the upside.
- *customer_acquisition*: A converting website and strong offer matter as much as traffic.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: Give high-touch staff (instructors) a share of upside because they own the customer relationship.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Allocate limited capital where customers perceive value; skip what they don't notice.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Simple does not mean easy; don't assume you can buy a range and do what the previous owner did.
- Topgolf-style hourly time rental and a surprise bill ruin the last five minutes and kill repeat visits.
- Assuming the market was only golfers was a mistake; families and non-golfers were a much bigger market.
- Water bills and land are huge, often surprising costs.
- Growing too fast on raised capital or franchising risks underperforming venues and losing standards.
- Giving up on surveys or ads after one weak attempt (10% response, $100 budget) is a common mistake.
