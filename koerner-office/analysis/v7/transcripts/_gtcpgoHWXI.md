## Tactics and playbook steps

**Validate the burger before committing to a restaurant** (validation_testing)
1. Make the product repeatedly at home and refine individual details.
2. Use friends and family requests as an early signal that people may pay for it.
3. Test the concept in a food trailer before signing a brick-and-mortar lease.
> "I like the food truck just because you can really test your concept before you make a massive decision like signing a lease on a brick and mortar spot." — Henry (~27:34)

**Learn restaurant operations by working across shifts** (operations)
1. Henry took a short job at Five Guys and worked a day shift, mid shifts, and a night shift to observe operations.
2. He later hired the manager he had worked under as Smashers' general manager.
> "I joined it intentionally, I called it Operation Trojan Burger, so I did a day shift, a mid shift, two mid shifts, and then a night shift" — Henry (~3:57)

**Look for second-generation restaurant sites** (operations)
1. Prefer a site previously used as a restaurant to reduce the amount of work needed.
2. Check visibility and whether customers can enter and exit easily.
3. Review household income and population within one-, three-, and five-mile radii.
4. Use an experienced real estate agent when reviewing the lease.
> "When looking for your first restaurant, you wanna make sure that's a second generation restaurant, the previous tenant to this was Fat Burger" — Henry (~4:33)

**Keep the menu simple and control food costs** (pricing)
1. Start with a narrow menu and add items only after the operation is established.
2. Track cost of goods and weekly inventory.
3. Compare suppliers and product quality instead of assuming the current vendor has the best deal.
4. Negotiate supplier pricing when buying power gives you leverage.
> "First things first, I'll keep my menu simple. I like the food truck just because you can really test your concept" — Henry (~27:32)
