## Summary

Chris Koerner visits Angela, a 20-year-old in Frisco, Texas, who runs 'Little Bit Bakery', a home micro bakery selling from a self-built cart on her parents' front porch every Saturday. She claims about $2,000-2,100 in sales per Saturday with roughly $500 in ingredient and packaging costs, selling out by about 9am, after a single local TikTok video took off. The video covers pricing, pre-orders via Hotplate, Texas cottage food law, a step-by-step start plan, and why hyper-local proximity businesses work. Key takeaway: a low-overhead, locally marketed home food business with weekly pre-orders can gross well, but it is very labour-intensive and physical.

### I1. Front-porch micro bakery (Little Bit Bakery)

- **Customer:** Neighbours and locals within about 15-40 minutes who want fresh bread and baked goods weekly
- **Problem:** Locals want fresh, high-quality artisan baked goods from a known local maker without going to a store or market
- **Offer:** Fresh sourdough, cinnamon rolls, muffins, bagels, English muffins, cookies, cake slices and sourdough starter, available once a week on Saturday via walk-up and pre-order
- **Business model:** One-off product sales per item, walk-up cash/Venmo/Zelle plus pre-orders, small tips
- **Pricing:** Per item or multi-pack pricing. classic 12, specialty 13-14 USD 12-14 per sourdough loaf; single USD 5 per cinnamon roll single; three-pack USD 15 per 3-pack cinnamon rolls; muffins USD 8 per 2-pack muffins; raised from 7 due to butter cost USD 8 per cake slice; quarter-pound cookie USD 5 per cookie; bagels USD 9 per 4-pack bagels; English muffins USD 8 per 4-pack English muffins; sourdough starter with instructions card USD 5 per jar Raised classic loaf from $11 to $12 with a slightly bigger loaf and no complaints; host suggests neighbours may pay a premium.
- **Acquisition:** organic_social_content: One 20-second POV TikTok ('You found a porch bakery in Frisco') posted overnight got about 100,000 local views and drove crowds; she films the build and behind-the-scenes process.; facebook_groups_communities: Nextdoor was her first big hit; also local Facebook groups.; referrals_word_of_mouth: Gave samples to family and friends for feedback and sharing; regulars pre-order every week.; other: Signage on her grandmother's front yard on a busy street pointing to the house; passers-by early on and a visible line attracting others.
- **Delivery:** Angela bakes everything herself in her parents' home kitchen (main bake Friday, cinnamon rolls and scones Saturday morning), starts at about 2:30-3:00am, stocks the cart from 7:30, sells 8-9am walk-up, and leaves pre-orders for pickup. Packaging and labelling are done by hand; mom and a friend occasionally help for pay.

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Film the build and behind-the-scenes to create a local audience** (acquisition)
1. Film building the cart and the baking process
2. Post consistently on TikTok and local channels
3. Post a short POV of the stand to attract locals
4. Tag or mention the neighbourhood so the audience is hyper-local
> "I always encourage to film like the building process of it, like invite people on that process because it makes them feel a part of it." — Angela (~7:37) [quote not matched verbatim]

**Launch and validate with friends, family and Nextdoor** (acquisition)
1. Check your state cottage food laws
2. Perfect recipes and give free samples to family and friends for feedback
3. Start selling to friends and family and ask them to share
4. Set up a simple table and post on Nextdoor and local Facebook groups
5. Add weekly pre-orders once repeat customers appear
> "I would start doing pre-orders as soon as you um just cuz that's going to make it easier on you on how much to make." — Angela (~24:36)

**Weekly pre-orders to de-risk weather and stock** (operations)
1. Open a pre-order menu online each week via Hotplate
2. Customers pay online at checkout
3. Set pre-orders out for pickup while walk-up is sold at the stand
4. Move to Sunday if the forecast shows 90% chance of rain
> "that's why I always recommend people doing some type of pre-order because again, it's a consistency that you get every single week" — Angela (~15:52)

**Capture zip codes on pre-orders to plan pop-ups** (operations)
1. Require a zip/postcode at pre-order
2. Look for clusters of customers by area
3. Run a pop-up market in the densest area
> "if you saw for whatever reason 70% of my pre-order come from 20 minutes away this neighborhood, like maybe it'd be worth trying to add another location" — Chris Koerner (~10:39)

**Raise prices gradually while increasing product size** (pricing)
1. Increase classic loaf from $11 to $12 while making it slightly bigger
2. Raise cake slice from $7 to $8 due to butter cost
3. Monitor for complaints
> "I actually started at 11 for my classic one. And then I recently went up to 12" — Angela (~12:06)

**Perfect one staple item first, then expand** (product)
1. Nail one recipe such as sourdough before selling
2. Prefer weekly-need staples like bread, bagels, English muffins over special-occasion cakes
3. Expand slowly into cinnamon rolls and other items
> "I would say nail down one recipe cuz I know I follow a lot of people, they just do sourdough" — Angela (~14:38)

## Lessons

- *customer_acquisition*: A single short, unpolished local video can create the whole initial demand; hyper-local reach is the most targeted audience.
- *customer_acquisition*: You don't need an existing audience; documenting the process builds one and makes customers feel part of the story.
- *offer_design*: Weekly scarcity and sell-outs plus visible queues create social proof and demand.
- *niche_selection*: Staple, repeat-need products (bread) beat special-occasion products (custom cakes) for consistent weekly revenue.
- *pricing*: Proximity and affinity let local sellers charge above market rates and win loyalty.
- *operations*: Low-overhead home operations avoid rent, but the real constraint is founder hours; the business is capped by one person's physical capacity.
- *operations*: Pre-orders reduce guesswork, weather risk and waste, and give guaranteed sales.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: Hiring hesitation over payroll can be solved by payroll software, and tracking even informal help shows true expenses.
- *validation_testing*: A cheap, simple start (folding table, friends and family feedback) validates before investing in a cart or equipment.
- *scaling*: Collecting customer location data can inform expansion or pop-up decisions.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Honour-system stand leads to occasional theft; verify pre-order QR codes rather than taking people's word.
- Check your state's cottage food laws first; in Texas a walk-up farm stand can't be left unattended.
- Packaging, labelling and prep time are heavily underestimated; burnout is a real risk.
- Test and perfect sourdough routine before selling it.
- Farmers markets can have high fees and category restrictions; her one day gave low profit.
- HOAs and neighbours might object if the stand ran daily; she limits it to early Saturdays.
