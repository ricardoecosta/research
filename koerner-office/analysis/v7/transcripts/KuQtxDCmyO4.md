## Tactics and playbook steps

**Start with a small local event and show up consistently** (customer_acquisition)
1. Set up a simple first market in a space you control.
2. Invite neighbors with flyers and offer a welcoming reason to attend, such as free drinks.
3. Repeat the event monthly so the community knows when to expect it.
4. Invite small makers and hobbyists to participate, then add activities that fit the event's mission.
> "And I decided I would be really consistent with it and just show up every month on a Saturday." — Hannah (~3:10)

**Use a personal story as the content** (customer_acquisition)
1. Explain why the product exists and what personal experience led to it.
2. Show the work and the process rather than only making a direct sales pitch.
3. Use comments and questions to explain the product to people who have not encountered it before.
4. Keep publishing and learn from how the audience responds.
> "I shared a lot about why I was doing it and where the idea came from." — Hannah (~5:17)

**Use a waitlist carefully** (sales)
1. Use a waitlist if demand exceeds the team's capacity.
2. State clearly on the signup form that the product costs money.
3. Tell people when signups will open and how long they may wait.
4. Measure how many waitlisted people actually buy before assuming the list represents demand.
> "I just threw up a little Google form like I wasn't even like thinking, but I didn't say that it cost money." — Hannah (~15:39)

**Protect the one-ounce mail limit** (operations)
1. Design each mailer around the weight restriction for the intended postage rate.
2. Weigh the final assembled envelope before dispatch.
3. Treat extra inserts and seasonal items as a postage decision, not only a creative decision.
> "the weight is what restricts us the most because I can't go over one ounce." — Hannah (~17:06)

**Batch and standardize packing** (operations)
1. Split the monthly mailer into repeatable steps such as stamping, addressing, inserting, and sealing.
2. Batch the work into a defined production week.
3. Bring in and train additional helpers when the original family group becomes overloaded.
4. Measure letters completed per batch and labor hours before deciding whether machinery is worthwhile.
> "This month is the first month we've like really standardized and brought in more people and that has like truly been a huge game changer." — Hannah (~17:34)

**Use unboxing posts as feedback** (product)
1. Watch subscriber unboxings and posts about the physical mailer.
2. Note what customers like, misunderstand, or share.
3. Use the feedback to improve future issues while keeping the product's creative direction.
> "I get to see in real time what they like and what they don't like and what they're confused by" — Hannah (~34:13)

**Track the source of new subscribers** (customer_acquisition)
1. Ask new customers which channel brought them to the product.
2. Offer the channels actually used, including Instagram, TikTok, and Pinterest, plus an other option.
3. Review answers to see where new signups are coming from.
> "We just added a question of like are you coming from Instagram, Tik Tok or Pinterest, which I would say and then other" — Hannah (~33:38)

**Test an archive shop** (product)
1. Keep previous issues available after the monthly dispatch.
2. Offer past issues to people who joined late or want a missed letter.
3. Test the archive as a one-off purchase or bundle before adding more production work.
> "I think having an archive shop is a really great way to you still have access to it, but it's still tangible goods and it's not virtual." — Hannah (~43:28)

**Keep monetization aligned with the mission** (pricing)
1. Check whether a new revenue stream changes how subscribers experience the product.
2. Decline advertising if it would make the mailer feel less personal or trustworthy.
3. Prefer improvements that make the existing product more valuable to its audience.
> "I had a company ask to put advertisements in and I'm pretty sure it would have been a great financial offer. But that completely deteriorates the entire mission behind it." — Hannah (~44:30)

**Consider physical mail as a client relationship touchpoint** (sales)
1. Look for businesses whose clients benefit from remembering the professional between transactions.
2. Develop a personal, useful mailer rather than inserting a generic advertisement.
3. Talk to a potential buyer before building a service package; the transcript mentions a realtor enquiry but no completed offer.
> "There are so many business owners that need that tangible touch point with their past clients, with new clients." — Chris Koerner (~27:31)

**Reverse-engineer a store's sales using order numbers** (validation_testing)
1. Place a test order in the store.
2. Place another order a week later and compare order numbers.
3. Use the difference as a rough estimate of weekly sales, then annualize it.
4. Treat the result as an estimate because order numbers may include non-sales or other changes.
> "you place another order exactly a week later. see that order number, subtract it, and you can calculate how many sales they're doing a week" — Chris Koerner (~11:26)

**Test niche versions of a letter product** (niche_selection)
1. Choose an audience with a clear interest, such as classic cars, golf, hunting, survival, or children's activities.
2. Make one sample issue for that audience before building a full subscription.
3. Use customer responses to decide whether to continue.
> "there's so many niches out there that these could be tailored to." — Hannah (~32:27)

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: A small local project can create community and a customer audience before it becomes a formal business.
- *customer_acquisition*: An authentic founder story can explain a product better than a generic sales pitch and can give an audience a reason to share it.
- *mindset_execution*: Test a small version of an idea quickly when the cost of trying it is low.
- *validation_testing*: A waitlist only measures buying intent when signups know the offer is paid and understand when they can buy.
- *customer_acquisition*: Track where customers discover the product so the founder can distinguish real acquisition sources.
- *offer_design*: Use customer behavior and feedback to improve the offer; subscriber unboxings showed Hannah what people liked and found confusing.
- *mindset_execution*: A business can stay small and still be meaningful; growth does not have to be the only measure of success.
- *operations*: Standardize repeatable work and add help when the original team becomes overloaded.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Physical details such as weight limits can determine whether a creative product remains economically viable to mail.
- *offer_design*: Protecting the promise and mission of a product can matter more than accepting every monetization opportunity.
- *customer_acquisition*: Be transparent about operational mistakes; Hannah said a candid post about a difficult production failure resonated with subscribers.
- *mindset_execution*: A founder can try business to learn whether it fits, even if they later return to employment.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Hannah said she panicked when the subscriber count reached 1,200 and closed signups; sudden social growth can outpace fulfillment capacity.
- A waitlist form that does not disclose the price can collect low-intent signups and inflate apparent demand.
- The monthly product must stay within the weight limit for its intended postage; an overweight issue led to restamping every letter.
- Red wine spilled over letters before a dispatch and created a difficult production problem.
- Hannah said her labor was a major expense and the workload was concentrated into one week each month.
- Hannah declined advertising in the mailer because she believed it would weaken the product's mission.
- A physical product has postage and production costs; the host's 70% gross-margin estimate explicitly excluded labor.
- Direct mail costs depend on the offer, business, targeting, and postal format; the host did not claim it works for every campaign.
- Hannah said the creative process and influence from similar artists made her cautious about consuming too much competing work.
- The host promoted HighLevel and TK Owners during the interview; these are promotional conflicts for the host.
