## Tactics and playbook steps

**Delegate with SMART instructions** (operations)
1. Specify the deliverable so the person knows exactly what to produce.
2. Set an objective measure that shows whether the work succeeded.
3. Check the person has the time, resources, skills, and authority to do it.
4. Connect the task to a real company priority and explain who benefits.
5. Set an exact deadline with a date and time; avoid vague words such as “soon.”
6. Return instructions that lack these elements to the person who wrote them for clarification.
> "So the first part of smart is specific and that means a clear deliverable." — Codie Sanchez (~7:07)

**Reframe a problem before accepting its proposed solution** (operations)
1. Identify the underlying problem before debating a proposed fix.
2. For capacity issues, examine low-value work and current constraints before approving additional headcount.
3. Park side issues and bring the discussion back to the decision that matters.
> "You do not want to fight inside somebody else's frame. This is a great lesson for life." — Codie Sanchez (~10:17)

**Speak in headlines and close with an action** (operations)
1. Start with the main point instead of a long preamble.
2. Keep the explanation concise and pause after making the point.
3. End each important conversation with a decision, a named owner, and a date.
4. Write the agreed action down so that deliverables and deadlines are clear.
> "Every important conversation that you have should end with three things. A decision, owner, and a date." — Codie Sanchez (~14:16)

**Bring a recommendation when escalating a problem** (customer_acquisition)
1. Describe the problem clearly.
2. Present the available options and your recommended choice.
3. Explain the risk of following the recommendation.
> "when you bring them a problem, bring them the options, your recommendation, and the risk of following it." — Codie Sanchez (~15:53)

**Take ownership when a decision goes wrong** (operations)
1. Say plainly that you made the call and underestimated the risk.
2. Set out who will correct the issue and when it will be reviewed.
3. Do not shift blame to the person who carried out an unclear instruction.
> "People will forgive your bad decisions. What they stop forgiving is watching a leader bend reality to protect their ego." — Codie Sanchez (~16:59)

**Use a hiring matrix to screen for evidence** (hiring)
1. Score candidates on whether they are known or referred, have done the exact job, understand the industry, have solved the specific problem, and have operated at the relevant scale.
2. Use the same criteria for each candidate instead of hiring out of fatigue or charm.
3. Codie says she is unlikely to hire someone scoring below 18 out of 25.
> "I've realized you don't actually have a hiring problem, you have a filtering problem." — Codie Sanchez (~19:53)

**Build a recruiting pool before making an offer** (hiring)
1. Find a company that already solves the problem you need solved.
2. Identify the people responsible for that work and build a broad candidate pool.
3. Use the 50-to-one rule described: review 50 profiles, interview 10 or sometimes 20, and make one offer.
4. Ask candidates to complete small paid work samples.
5. Call people who have directly observed the candidate's work rather than relying only on written references.
> "I also like to require work samples, use small paid projects, we'll pay people to do the work, and call people who have watched the candidate perform not the ones that are written on their resume." — Codie Sanchez (~22:20)

**Tie incentives to measurable outcomes** (finance)
1. Pay salespeople for collected revenue and retained customers rather than call volume.
2. Reward content teams for outcomes such as reviews, watch time, and follower growth rather than the number of pieces published.
3. For senior operators, consider profit sharing or earned equity tied to measurable value created.
> "So do not reward people for looking productive. Pay them for moving the scoreboard." — Codie Sanchez (~24:16)

**Protect team time by removing low-value meetings** (operations)
1. Cancel meetings that have no decision to make, owner to assign, or problem to solve.
2. When a meeting is needed, use an agenda and finish early.
3. Remove people who do not need to attend so they can return to their work.
> "If it has no decision to make, owner to assign or problem to solve, kill the meeting." — Codie Sanchez (~25:33)

**Recognize specific behavior while the win is fresh** (hiring)
1. Use the existing company-wide meeting rhythm to recognize people.
2. Name the person, the result, and the behavior that produced it.
3. Use a wins channel and specific recurring awards so the team sees what work is valued.
> "So make the right behavior impossible to miss." — Codie Sanchez (~26:19)

**Set standards and explain consequences in advance** (operations)
1. Tell employees clearly when peak periods require them to be available.
2. Explain the reason for blackout dates and give people a chance to discuss workable alternatives.
3. Apply the same standards consistently so exceptions do not undermine accountability.
> "Every exception you tolerate becomes permission. Let someone miss the same standard twice and the third one's on you." — Codie Sanchez (~27:54)

## Lessons

- *mindset_execution*: Leadership is a set of learnable skills that raises the performance ceiling of a team.
- *mindset_execution*: Trust and honest feedback matter more than being liked by everyone.
- *operations*: Delegate outcomes with clear measures, authority, relevance, and deadlines so people can act without repeatedly asking for direction.
- *operations*: Good communication starts with the point and ends with a decision, owner, and date.
- *mindset_execution*: When raising a problem, bring options, a recommendation, and the risk of that recommendation.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: Hire for evidence that matches the actual role, problem, industry, and company scale.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Reward measurable results rather than visible activity, and connect senior upside to value created.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: Protect high performers' time and give them meaningful responsibility, access, and recognition.
- *scaling*: The owner's role shifts from doing tasks to setting judgment, vision, and a consistent example.
- *automation_ai*: Build systems that preserve the business when a key person leaves.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Trying to please everyone can lead to committee decisions and leadership that seeks approval instead of trust.
- A leader who repeatedly rescues missed deadlines and bad work can create a team that relies on the leader to fix its mistakes.
- Calling a company a family can blur performance expectations and make it harder to address poor performance.
- Doing every job personally can burn out the founder and make the business dependent on one person.
- Vague assignments can result in technically correct work that misses the intended outcome.
- Codie argues that leaders should regulate emotional displays in front of their teams; this is a forceful personal stance rather than a demonstrated business result.
- Hiring after a tiring search can lead to choosing a charming candidate without enough evidence.
- Tolerating repeated exceptions can weaken standards and make later enforcement the leader's responsibility.
- A company can become fragile when important decisions and knowledge remain in the founder's head.
