4. Ask reception to send an internal message about the visit.
5. Use successful visits to build recognition and event bookings.
> "Let's say the first three offices Monday, Wednesday, Friday, then office number one, 10 to 11, then office number two, 11 to 12, office number three, 12 to one." — Vlad Priadko (~50:49)

**Improve event booth conversion** (sales)
1. Apply to larger events after collecting examples from smaller markets.
2. Ask an organizer to deduct the booth fee from event proceeds if upfront cash is a barrier.
3. Display a variety of past photos where passersby can see them.
4. Test larger visuals to catch attention from farther away.
5. Treat each event as a chance to build future referrals and bookings.
> "I've got like 20 or 30 out. I try to pick them that look very different. So it invites curiosity." — Summer Ray (~56:42)

**Qualify vending locations before placing machines** (acquisition)
1. Visit a building and ask for the general manager or property manager.
2. Ask about current vending and whether it meets residents' or employees' needs.
3. Ask about unit or employee counts to qualify potential foot traffic.
4. Target locations with at least 100 residential units or 100 employees, according to Mike.
5. Look for broken machines, lack of card payments, or providers that do not keep machines stocked.
> "Do you want to provide modern amenities? And then you just start asking questions about foot traffic to qualify them." — Mike Hoffman (~59:50)

**Price vending location quality by customer base** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Estimate the number of people served, including the number of residents per unit.
2. Check whether payment systems accept cards.
3. Use remote sales and inventory monitoring to restock before customer-facing stockouts.
4. Compare the expected sales opportunity with physical restocking and machine maintenance time.
> "Like, okay, we've sold eight out of 10 salads to left. We need to backfill those salads." — Mike Hoffman (~61:21)

## Lessons

- *customer_acquisition*: Start by finding an existing group of buyers rather than building a product and hoping people discover it.
- *niche_selection*: Marketplace demand is useful only when the product is specific enough to stand out among competitors.
- *validation_testing*: Treat marketplace search data as a way to choose where to compete, especially when demand is present and competition is manageable.
- *customer_acquisition*: Selling on a platform can provide reach, but a brand's own audience can reduce dependence on platform traffic and commodity pricing.
- *validation_testing*: Use a marketplace gig as a low-friction test of whether customers want a service before expecting premium pricing.
- *niche_selection*: One strong, unexpected sale can reveal demand for items that look obsolete in the seller's local market.
- *niche_selection*: A narrow product line can be more compelling than a broad collection when a clearly defined group has a recurring need.
- *offer_design*: Build products from real work or expertise you already have, then test whether other people value them.
- *validation_testing*: Choose physical locations based on customers' willingness to stop and buy, not just on footfall.
- *customer_acquisition*: Recurring placements and internal communication can turn a mobile service into repeat visits and event referrals.
- *mindset_execution*: Early work will be imperfect; improve the offer and presentation through repeated attempts rather than waiting until everything feels polished.
- *operations*: In marketplace resale, consistency and refreshed listings can matter as much as obtaining inventory.
- *automation_ai*: Digital products can create repeat sales from one piece of work, while physical services still require human execution.
- *operations*: The simplest operational technology can improve service quality when it reveals inventory before a machine is visibly empty.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Marketplace reach does not eliminate the need to understand the platform's fees, rules, and physical operating costs.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Do not assume a generic product will be discovered: the host warns that a broad thank-you card listing has very high Etsy competition.
- Amazon private-label selling has become more competitive, fee-heavy, and dependent on paid visibility.
- Amazon can launch a competing basic product and put pressure on a seller's price and brand.
- Voiceover sellers may undercharge if they do not understand licensing; Fiverr's fee also reduced the first gig's proceeds.
- Poplin providers carry the cost of mileage and utilities, and early platform rankings may limit order flow.
- ShareTown resale requires physical pickup and storage, and listings may stagnate without consistent refreshing.
- Dress rental demand depends on recent styles, size, brand, season, and customer segment; the operator avoids expensive bags because she self-insures.
- High foot traffic does not guarantee sales if people are rushing and unwilling to stop.
- Vending sales depend heavily on location, and older providers may fail to restock or accept card payments.
- The host describes long hours and repeated effort finding office customers for the mobile coffee business.
