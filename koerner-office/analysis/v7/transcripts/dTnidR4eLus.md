## Summary

Chris Koerner and Brandon Doyle walk through five offline side hustles framed around making $10K in a year, month, week or day: temporary construction fence rental, portable mini-mini-golf (and cornhole golf) rentals/installs, inflatable outdoor movie rentals, buying local original art abroad to resell, and flipping a pallet of auction rugs. The fullest case is Brandon's teenage son, who bought 115 auction rugs for about $1,500 and sold about 80 for over $3,000 to fund a first car. The fence story is second-hand and the others are mostly observed or hypothetical. The takeaway is that low-tech, rent-or-resell businesses have cheap entry and fast payback, but almost all need physical work.

### I1. Temporary construction fence rental

- **Customer:** Construction site managers and general contractors on new builds without fencing.
- **Problem:** Sites legally or for liability reasons need perimeter fencing, and builders do not want to buy it.
- **Offer:** Delivery, set-up and removal of temporary fence panels at a low rental rate.
- **Business model:** Rental of owned panels, priced per linear foot per month with discounts for longer terms; deposits upfront.
- **Pricing:** Per linear foot per month, tapering with duration. starting rate USD 4 per linear foot (per month, as assumed); long-term rate USD 2 per linear foot (longer rentals); average job value claimed USD 20000-30000 per job Hosts assumed the rates are monthly; contract total may be paid in instalments or 50-75% upfront.
- **Acquisition:** door_to_door_in_person: Friend drove around, found new construction sites without fencing, went to the site trailer and pitched the person in charge. Hosts also suggest calling the number on the contractor's banner.; cold_call: Call the number on the construction banner; also idea of using public building permits to get ahead of site start.
- **Delivery:** Owner and crew set up fence in about a day and remove it months later; no maintenance or cleaning in between.
- **Unit economics (claims):** Average job $20-30K; Sold the company for $600K after 18 months; Several hundred thousand made before sale
- **Tools:** Public building permit records
- **Startup cost:** Cost of buying panels; idea is to sell the first job and then buy fencing, so possibly near zero upfront.
- **Time to first revenue:** Quick: sell first job, then buy panels and install within a day
- **UK applicability [E]:** 4/5 — Hoarding and Heras-style fence hire already exists in the UK; local planning rules and the incumbent hire companies differ but the model carries over.
- **Risks:** Large upfront capex for panels; Second-hand claim from a friend; Incumbent fence hire firms; Payment is spread over months, so cash flow is slower than headline job value
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 1/5 (Site visits are in person; permit-data lead lists and calling businesses could be partly automated, but the friend's route is physical.); onboarding 2/5 (Quotes and contracts can be templated and e-signed, with a human confirming site details.); delivery 0/5 (Trucks, crews and physical panel installation and removal.); billing 4/5 (Invoicing and instalment billing is easily automated.); support 2/5 (Occasional site issues need a person on site.)

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Sell the job before buying inventory** (finance)
1. Pitch and close the first construction site job
2. Take a deposit upfront (possibly 50-75%)
3. Use the deposit to buy the fence panels
4. Install in about a day
> "don't go buy your fencing first. Sell your first job. Yeah. Then go buy your fencing." — Chris Koerner (~8:11)

**Find fence leads from signage and permits** (acquisition)
1. Notice sites about to start while driving
2. Call the number on the contractor's banner
3. Mention you have the best rates in the city and ask who to talk to
4. Optionally monitor public building permits to reach sites weeks ahead

**Duration-tiered pricing** (pricing)
1. Charge about $4 per linear foot
2. Reduce towards $2 per foot for longer rentals
3. Expect jobs to overrun to your advantage

**Remote art sourcing via photos and preorders** (operations)
1. Find local shops on Google
2. Call and ask for close-up photos by WhatsApp
3. List on Facebook Marketplace at a markup
4. Collect deposits and warn of 2-4 week delivery
5. Then pay the shop and ship rolled in a tube

**Clearance-lot flipping with a worst-case floor** (finance)
1. Check local Marketplace prices first
2. Buy only if worst-case average resale beats cost
3. Sell via Marketplace plus a themed garage sale with road signs
4. Upsell low-value items such as doormats at the sale

**Photograph inventory well** (sales)
1. Roll the item out on a clean indoor background
2. Use one rug turned upside down as a backdrop for others
3. Avoid grass or dirt backgrounds

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Teach young people via small asset-light flips with a capped downside; set the worst-case resale price above cost before buying.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Rental assets with cheap equipment pay back in a few rentals, then become mostly profit.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Selling before buying inventory removes capital risk.
- *customer_acquisition*: Free marketplaces can be the entire acquisition channel for local rental or resale.
- *offer_design*: Competing on a simple trust promise (clean every time) can differentiate in sloppy industries.
- *niche_selection*: Cross-border arbitrage has endless combinations of countries and product types because markets are never fully efficient.
- *customer_acquisition*: In-person sales help conversion and upsells, as seen at the garage sale.
- *mindset_execution*: Hands-on selling teaches photography, negotiation, pricing and upselling skills.
- *customer_acquisition*: Content that reaches an algorithm-matched audience can work even with few views.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Rug buyer did no demand research before buying and had to hold heavy stock in storage; winter is the worst selling season.
- Pre-selling art remotely risks selling something that is then sold elsewhere, requiring refunds.
- Passive rental businesses can become boring to run, leading to early sale of the company.
