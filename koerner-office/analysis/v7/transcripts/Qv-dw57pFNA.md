## Tactics and playbook steps

**Start from an unfair advantage** (product)
1. List audience access, work history, skills and experiences that give you insight into a market.
2. Choose an offer that uses one of those existing advantages.
3. For Chris, that was a newsletter he could sell to.
> "anytime anyone starts a business, you need to go to your unfair advantage. Everyone has an unfair advantage." — Chris Koerner (~3:09)

**Launch a minimum offer before polishing the brand** (validation_testing)
1. Choose a workable name and move forward without hiring a branding agency or conducting a friend survey.
2. Create a payment link and write the first sales email.
3. Aim to get a first customer quickly so real sales data can guide improvements.
> "I just want to launch. I want revenue. That's what I preach, right? You guys have heard me say that. Get your first customer as soon as possible." — Chris Koerner (~10:17)

**Randomize subscribers before an email price test** (validation_testing)
1. Export active subscribers and paste them into a spreadsheet.
2. Use a random number formula, sort by that value, and remove the formula column.
3. Split a small sample into groups so older or more loyal subscribers do not skew one test group.
4. Use separate tags or segments for each price group.
> "I want to perfectly randomize this list because what I'm about to do is what's called an AB test or a split test." — Chris Koerner (~6:05)

**Test prices with identical email copy** (pricing)
1. Send the same subject line and email copy to equal-sized audience samples.
2. Change only the payment link and price between the groups.
3. Compare sales and revenue per subscriber, then allow time for higher-priced buyers to decide.
4. Refund buyers if a later test establishes that they paid more than the final test price.
> "If I send both of those identical emails, it's the same email, same subject, same wording, same everything. The only thing that's different is the payment link with the price" — Chris Koerner (~13:05)

**Reduce checkout friction** (sales)
1. Ask only for information needed to complete the purchase.
2. Do not add fields such as names or phone numbers if the seller already has the information or does not need it.
3. Test whether an external community checkout adds account-creation friction compared with a Stripe payment link.
> "The more friction you add to the checkout process, the more your conversion rate will drop. I don't care what it is you're selling. Do not add friction." — Chris Koerner (~14:26)

**Show the price before the click** (sales)
1. Test short emails with the price stated near the link against emails where the price is hidden until checkout.
2. Use separate payment links to track each variant.
3. In Chris's test, the version that disclosed the price performed better, so he chose to state the price in the full-list email.
> "So the lesson, tell them the price in the email. That's what we're going to do here." — Chris Koerner (~40:46)

**Track each checkout link separately** (operations)
1. Create separate Stripe payment links for each test.
2. Use Stripe payment-link analytics to compare results when the price is the same but the checkout destination or email copy differs.
3. Exclude people who already received a test email from later sends.
> "If we want to see how many people are using each of the payment links, we go into the payment link in question and then we click payments and analytics." — Chris Koerner (~35:30)

**Repurpose existing material into a paid library** (product)
1. Inventory existing training videos, guides, documents and tracking spreadsheets.
2. Package useful material as an initial paid offer.
3. Organize the files into a cleaner course or community interface after validating demand.
> "we basically downloaded all of the Google Drive content and uploaded it here uh into separate folders." — Chris Koerner (~38:46)

**Use a repeatable digital fulfillment flow** (operations)
1. Take payment through a Stripe link.
2. Redirect buyers to the hosted content or automatically invite them into the community.
3. Use a short welcome video and clear folders to orient new members.
4. Record live AMAs so people who miss them can still access the material.
> "I can still automatically invite or add them to school after they pay on Stripe. I already have the automation set up." — Chris Koerner (~43:02)

**Stand behind the value of a course** (offer_design)
1. Make a clear promise about what the buyer gets.
2. Ensure the product provides the promised value.
3. Use the material to help buyers take action rather than selling unsupported outcomes.
> "They only have a bad rap because so many of them don't match the value that the salesperson promises." — Chris Koerner (~47:41)

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Start with an advantage you already have, such as audience access, job experience or a useful insight into a market.
- *validation_testing*: Get a real customer quickly; early revenue can validate an offer and reveal what to improve.
- *mindset_execution*: Avoid spending too long on branding or analysis before trying a simple offer with customers.
- *validation_testing*: Use a randomized audience sample and change one variable at a time to learn from email tests.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Consider revenue per subscriber and the quality of buyers, not only the number of conversions.
- *sales*: Give buyers enough information to decide before they click; in Chris's test, disclosing the price outperformed hiding it.
- *offer_design*: Reduce unnecessary steps in checkout, because each extra step can cause some buyers to leave.
- *offer_design*: Use existing useful material as a product instead of assuming every offer needs new content production.
- *customer_acquisition*: A small audience can still support a business if the creator builds a close relationship and offers relevant paid products.
- *offer_design*: Build trust by delivering the value promised; a course buyer may be more likely to act after consuming the material.
- *legal_risk*: Do not rely on one platform as the sole source of income; Chris's Facebook ban removed an important revenue stream.

## Warnings and mistakes

- A business that depends on one social platform can lose income suddenly when the platform removes an account. Chris says Facebook banned his page and did not reinstate it.
- Chris says dependence on his Facebook followers also made his ads harder to run profitably after the ban.
- Do not make a course promise that the content cannot meet; Chris says this mismatch is why courses have a bad reputation.
- Do not assume that a high-priced offer converts better or that a low price is always best; Chris recommends testing prices against a finite audience.
- Avoid adding unnecessary checkout fields or account-creation steps, since Chris believes added friction reduces conversion.
- The transcript describes emails to active newsletter subscribers; replicating this with unsolicited marketing to UK individuals or sole traders would raise PECR consent requirements.
