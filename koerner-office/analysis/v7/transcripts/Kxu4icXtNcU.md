## Summary

David Heacock, founder of Filterbuy (US air filter manufacturer, claimed ~150,000 filters/day and ~$25M/month revenue), shares five stages of building a boring business: commit long enough to learn, fund the constraint, build systems that preserve judgment, don't diversify before the core is ready, and build resilience and legacy. He recounts leaving Goldman, buying a declining $300k/yr business with debt, taking $50k salary for 7.5 years, and losing millions in 2021 by launching a freight company and buying an HVAC business while the core was doubling. Key takeaway: do the work yourself first, turn judgment into systems, and put capital into the core constraint rather than diversifying early.

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Do it manually, build tool, hand off, then iterate** (operations)
1. Do the critical weekly job yourself manually longer than feels comfortable to learn the tradeoffs
2. Build a rough tool that encodes how you make the decision
3. Train a person to run the tool daily without you
4. Watch, give feedback and repeat until they outperform you
> "I would do the job myself manually long enough that I understood not just the steps, but the tradeoffs that I was making" — David Heacock (~10:22)

**Fund the current constraint** (finance)
1. Identify whether sales or operations is currently the bottleneck
2. Put available cash and attention into that constraint
3. Swing back when the other side becomes the bottleneck
4. Keep experiments small (small cost, about a week of attention)
> "you just have to follow whichever half of the business is breaking" — David Heacock (~4:23)

**Four-question diversification test** (finance)
1. Does the core have a management team that can operate it?
2. Do you have a real framework or advantage that transfers to the new area?
3. Does the new initiative reinforce the long-term vision rather than satisfy novelty?
4. Is the core already receiving all the capital and attention it can productively use?
> "the core business is already receiving all the capital and attention it can productively use" — David Heacock (~17:32)

**Choose a boring product by durability** (product)
1. Shortlist products you can make competitively locally and sell direct
2. Ask what will not change in 10-20 years
3. Prefer products with a mediocre existing customer experience
> "I chose based on which boring product would still exist in 20 years." — David Heacock (~2:13)

## Lessons

- *mindset_execution*: Commitment and long runway let you afford to be wrong; keeping personal pay low funded years of mistakes.
- *mindset_execution*: Always preserving options has a cost; building something meaningful requires closing doors.
- *niche_selection*: Pick boring products whose demand will persist so knowledge compounds over decades.
- *operations*: A real system preserves judgment, not just a checklist of activities.
- *scaling*: Momentum is not proof you can do anything; put extra capital into the core while it can still absorb it.
- *operations*: Aim for a resilient company rather than one that needs no founder; absence is not the goal.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Staying private and self-controlled allows decade-long thinking instead of quarterly pressure.
- *automation_ai*: Be willing to rebuild systems as technology like AI changes what is possible.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Diversifying (freight + HVAC) while the core was doubling cost millions and years of cleanup.
- Delegating before you've figured out the work yourself leaves you unable to judge or give feedback.
- Systems built so you can stop paying attention break when reality changes.
- Estate planning gets more expensive after value is created; do it early.
