## Tactics and playbook steps

**Validate local demand with paid ads before committing to premises** (validation_testing)
1. Choose a tight geographic radius around the intended site.
2. Use the platform's audience targeting for people living in that radius, rather than assuming interest from broad demographics.
3. Run similar creative variations with different lead-form paths.
4. Measure both cost per lead and how many prospects complete the longer survey.
5. Ask about likely usage, preferred times, singles versus doubles, willingness to pay, and requested features before expanding the facility.
> "I wanted real people in this area to tell me would they pay for this most importantly how often do they want to play" — Nick (~4:20)

**Collect contact details before asking longer survey questions** (acquisition)
1. Use a short initial form to collect name and email.
2. Send interested prospects to a longer Typeform survey.
3. Compare instant-form leads with people who finish the longer survey.
4. Use a clear reason to continue the form, such as the promised free item.
> "the best bang for your buck is I capture their name and their email before I get anything else" — Nick (~3:52)

**Make a local ad feel specific and exclusive** (acquisition)
1. Name nearby towns so local viewers recognize the service area.
2. State the opening date, access, amenities, and membership cap.
3. Use concise creative the founder can make without waiting on editors.
> "secret pickup ball opens on July 30th we're capping the memberships 24/7 key card access reserve a court" — Nick (~5:17)

**Test what members want before setting capacity** (validation_testing)
1. Ask current and intended play frequency, preferred time of day, and singles versus doubles.
2. Ask whether the proposed price is acceptable and how likely respondents are to join.
3. Ask how many friends they might bring and what features or pricing changes they want.
4. Use the answers to estimate how many members the facility can support.
> "if everyone plays singles then I can only have half as many members if they all play doubles I can have twice as many members" — Nick (~4:33)

**Use customer suggestions to improve the offer** (offer_design)
1. Ask what the club should add or change.
2. Record requests such as a room to practice dinks or a ball-serving machine.
3. Check whether each feature appears often enough in responses to justify its cost.
> "people were like "Oh I'd like a room to practice my dinks." Or "There's this $2,000 machine" — Nick (~8:32)

**Use local SEO for a niche home service** (acquisition)
1. Check how many actual local providers appear for the service, separating refinishing businesses from remodelers.
2. Build the service around a clear lower-cost alternative customers may not know exists.
3. Prioritize organic search if it is already producing the operator's best leads.
4. Consider a lead-generation site or directory only after confirming contractor demand.

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Test the offer with real local prospects before committing to a larger facility or expensive expansion.
- *validation_testing*: Ask prospects operational questions, not just whether they like the idea; usage patterns determine capacity and economics.
- *customer_acquisition*: A small lead count can still be useful when the respondents show strong intent and fit a narrow local market.
- *niche_selection*: A niche service can be attractive when it solves an expensive problem with a lower-cost alternative and has few local competitors.
- *validation_testing*: Prefer real-time customer evidence over static market research when the key question is whether people nearby will actually buy.
- *offer_design*: Use a clear, affordable alternative to explain why a customer should choose a service instead of the more expensive incumbent option.
- *automation_ai*: Accountability can be stronger when there is a real person expecting follow-through, so test whether automated reminders can create enough commitment.
- *automation_ai*: A useful AI product for professionals may save time on repeated review work while leaving the professional responsible for decisions.
