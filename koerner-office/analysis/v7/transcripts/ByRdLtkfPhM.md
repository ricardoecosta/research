## Tactics and playbook steps

**Value a company from an acquirer's perspective** (valuation)
1. Estimate what a buyer could pay for the whole business.
2. Compare that estimate with the public-market price.
3. Look for a significant discount to the estimated acquisition value.
> "We look what would uh if someone was able to buy this whole business, what would they pay?" — Jonathan Boyar (~1:35)

**Build a margin of safety and wait** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Compare the expected return with the risk-free rate.
2. Require a higher return for a more speculative business.
3. Be prepared for a price to fall further and stick to the investment philosophy.
> "you have to be patient and stick to a philosophy that has worked really well over time." — Jonathan Boyar (~11:52)

**Find ideas through focused research** (product)
1. Use experienced analysts to read and look for businesses rather than relying only on generic screening tools.
2. Look for companies through the lens of what an acquirer would buy.
3. Focus on a few strong ideas each year instead of forcing constant activity.
> "You only need a few ideas a year. So, how do you think about return on capital?" — Jonathan Boyar (~13:36)

**Make a catalyst part of the research report** (product)
1. Explain what the business is worth.
2. Identify what could unlock that value and make the stock rise over a reasonable period.
3. Check for value-trap risk before publishing an idea.
> "what's the catalyst what's going to make that stock ascend in value over a reasonable period of time" — Jonathan Boyar (~25:31)

**Package research for multiple budgets** (pricing)
1. Offer an expensive full research service for professional investors.
2. Create a lower-priced Substack with less detailed reports and one stock idea a month.
3. Sell selected annual research a la carte.
> "we started a Substack, you know, lower lower information reports, very good, same ideas or one stock a month that investors get" — Jonathan Boyar (~26:50)

**Use regular research releases as a product schedule** (operations)
1. Publish six issues a year with three company profiles in each issue.
2. Add a summer update on about 15 names.
3. Publish an annual Forgotten 40 list with one-page snapshots.
> "we publish um you know six times a year we come out with this issue where we profile three companies. So obviously that's 18 companies" — Jonathan Boyar (~25:14)

**Use media and referrals to reach investors** (acquisition)
1. Build a reputation by doing good work for existing clients.
2. Ask satisfied clients to refer others.
3. Appear on relevant podcasts and other media, and use an owned podcast to reach prospective clients.
> "we go on podcasts like this. We're we're in Barns. We're in all sorts of other media. I have my own podcast." — Jonathan Boyar (~28:15)

**Dollar-cost average to make investing easier to stick with** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Decide how much money can be invested without compromising near-term living needs.
2. Divide a lump sum into regular installments across a year.
3. Follow the schedule and avoid reacting to headlines.
> "Just be systematic. Don't look at the headlines." — Jonathan Boyar (~29:42)

**Look where the market is not looking** (niche_selection)
1. Search in hated areas or smaller companies when popular stocks look expensive.
2. Write down the valuation gap and the specific catalyst that could close it.
3. Use media attention to make the case when there is a concrete corporate action, as Boyar did in a letter to James Dolan.
> "you go where others aren't? You go what's what's hated. You have to be very careful or you go into kind of these smaller names." — Jonathan Boyar (~32:27)

**Check advice-giver incentives** (legal)
1. Before following financial advice, identify how the adviser is paid.
2. Ask whether the product recommendation benefits the adviser or the client.
3. Consider whether advice fits the investor's goals and need for cash.
> "You just have to be very careful of um the people giving you advice. You want to look at what the incentives are" — Jonathan Boyar (~30:10)

## Lessons

- *finance_unit_economics*: Value a business by considering what a full-company buyer might pay, then require a meaningful discount to that estimate.
- *pricing*: A good business still can be a poor investment if its current price is too high.
- *mindset_execution*: Investors should understand their own tolerance for losses and choose an investing schedule they can maintain.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Use the risk-free rate as a reference point and demand more return for more speculative investments.
- *validation_testing*: Look for a catalyst that could close a valuation gap; a cheap-looking company can remain cheap without one.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Cash flow matters to business owners because it supports day-to-day life, even when paper valuations attract attention.
- *automation_ai*: Do not adopt a tool's output without checking it; use AI as a source of ideas only when facts can be verified.
- *niche_selection*: Long-term ownership requires considering whether the business is one you would be willing to own through changing market conditions.
- *exit_acquisition*: A business sale is not automatically a better outcome; consider what the owner will do afterward and whether ongoing cash flow suits their goals.
- *legal_risk*: Understand the incentives of anyone giving financial advice before acting on it.
