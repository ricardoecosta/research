## Tactics and playbook steps

**Test the offer with a simple local post** (validation_testing)
1. Describe one clear woodworking class on a personal Facebook profile.
2. Answer questions about ages, price, date, and venue as they come in.
3. Offer a small number of free sessions if needed to get initial photos, videos, and reviews.
> "So yeah, Thanksgiving week I was like we need money and Facebook post woodworking birthday parties." — Mike Herod (~1:42)

**Collect payment before buying the first tools** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Set up a simple payment link with Square or Stripe.
2. Ask customers to pay in full or place a deposit before the class.
3. Use advance payments to fund the initial equipment and materials.
> "They pay full out and use that three to five 700 dollars to frontload your first set of tools and then from there you're just repeating." — Mike Herod (~15:40)

**Use community groups and daily project photos** (customer_acquisition)
1. Post in local homeschool groups and parents' groups.
2. Share project photos consistently so busy parents see the offer and the results.
3. Ask satisfied families to share the classes through their existing community.
> "But it's all been Facebook. But, you know, listen to you and others posting consistently. Like, I have to post every single day." — Mike Herod (~26:46)

**Sell the outcome parents care about** (offer_design)
1. Lead with the confidence children gain from making something.
2. Describe the project and the benefit in plain language.
3. Use a simple image and invite parents to message for details.
> "we don't teach woodworking. We help kids gain confidence through tools and woodworking." — Mike Herod (~36:56)

**Keep projects simple and repeatable** (operations)
1. Choose projects that use basic cuts, screws, and glue.
2. Prepare kits in advance and store them in labelled totes.
3. Reuse a small set of proven projects, while letting returning students choose a prior project.
> "But really, I've got 10 projects, Chris, and that'll keep us busy for probably five years." — Mike Herod (~33:30)

**Set safety rules before every build** (operations)
1. Start each class with the shop rules and eye protection.
2. Set a firm boundary around tool use and do not waive it.
3. Match power tools to age and experience; younger children can use hand tools.
> "Draw the line in the sand with safety and don't move an itch. Safety glasses." — Mike Herod (~31:31)

**Use large-group pricing when selling to an organiser** (pricing)
1. Ask the organiser how long the session should run and how many students will attend.
2. Set a group minimum and maximum that match staffing and equipment capacity.
3. Price a contract separately from one-off per-child classes.
> "And basically, I step down the the pricing, it's not, you know, $50 an hour. And basically tear it down basically, you know, like now our big ones is 30 kids minimum." — Mike Herod (~27:35)

**Create a separate vendor payment path for eligible families** (operations)
1. Register as an approved vendor in the applicable scholarship program.
2. Have the parent book the class and enter the code that zeroes out the checkout cart.
3. Direct the parent to submit the payment request through the program portal.
> "They register on our line for the class, right, or book it and then they put a code in that zeros out the cart" — Mike Herod (~18:05)

**Ask suppliers for sponsorship using customer lifetime value** (sales)
1. Contact a relevant tool supplier through a business contact on LinkedIn.
2. Explain how early tool exposure could build future customers for the supplier.
3. Use a customer lifetime value estimate in the pitch and ask directly for sponsorship.

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Test a simple offer with people already in the founder's local network before investing in a venue or elaborate setup.
- *customer_acquisition*: A small free trial can create the photos, reviews, and referrals needed to sell later sessions.
- *offer_design*: Describe the result the customer values, such as a child's confidence, rather than only naming the activity.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Collect payment up front so the first bookings can fund the initial equipment.
- *operations*: A small set of repeatable projects can be enough to sustain a recurring class schedule.
- *niche_selection*: The right venue can affect perceived trust and safety; Herod says families responded differently once they saw the commercial space.
- *legal_risk*: Keep safety rules clear and consistent when the service involves children and tools.
- *operations*: Use equipment suited to the learners and replaceable consumables; children may drop, stain, or break tools.
- *customer_acquisition*: Talk to potential sponsors with a concrete customer lifetime value argument and ask directly.
- *customer_acquisition*: The guest's account ties repeat demand to a tight-knit community and consistent sharing of real class outcomes.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Safety is a non-negotiable: Herod says one injury could wreck the business. He requires eye protection and starts classes with safety rules.
- Avoid projects that are too complex for the session. Herod says early classes ran long when he added paint and glue, then he simplified the projects.
- Herod advises against using nails with children because he considers them an injury risk; he uses screws instead.
- Parents may decline permission for photos. Use clear waivers and respect requests to leave a child out of posted images.
- Herod says he initially overbought tools because he did not know what the classes needed; he recommends starting with a basic kit and borrowing where possible.
- Herod says cheap lumber made the experience worse and later switched to higher-grade pine.
- Florida's Step Up scholarship payments are location-specific; the episode's 24-hour payment claim should not be assumed to apply to UK funding.
- The host and guest discuss the business in a promotional setting: the host solicits podcast applicants and paid community memberships, while Herod says Milwaukee supplied free tools.
