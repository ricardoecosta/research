## Summary

Chris Koerner interviews Forest, a 34-year-old former Pepsi/Coke/Bang merchandiser and beverage brand rep. He bought a failed pastry route for $15k, grew it to about $800k a year and sold it in two parts for about $185k. He then moved to Billings, Montana and started a small-distributor business selling jerky, candy and novelty snacks to independent gas stations and grocery stores. He reports about 95 accounts, roughly $50k a month in sales, 30% net margin and one lost account. The takeaway is that a hands-on, relationship-based route business that merchandises and buys back unsold stock, using local products, net-10 customer terms and net-14 to net-30 supplier terms, can start with very little capital. It is physical, driving-heavy work, not agent-run.

### I1. Independent-store jerky and candy micro-distributor (merchandising route)

- **Customer:** Independent gas stations, small grocery stores, hardware stores, lumber yards, liquor stores and auto parts stores, mostly rural; small regional chains (e.g. 8-store and 20-store accounts).
- **Problem:** Big wholesalers drop product and leave, with no upselling or relationship, no regional or local products, and store owners carry the mental load of ordering and forecasting inventory. Out-of-stocks and outdated stock are not bought back.
- **Offer:** Visit every other week, place the order, merchandise and rotate the shelf, take over whole sections (e.g. jerky), supply products the big wholesaler does not carry (especially local brands), buy back credits on unsold or outdated items, and supply free manufacturer racks and displays. Positioned as an addition to the store's existing wholesaler.
- **Business model:** Wholesale resale margin: buy from manufacturers, mark up 30-45%, sell by the case to stores on net-10 terms. No service fee.
- **Pricing:** Per-case wholesale with 30-45% markup over cost, net-10 customer terms. typical order USD 500-900 per gas station delivery (summer); example order USD 1000 per example order (about $650-700 cost); best grocery account USD 3500 per delivery at best grocery store; average account USD 500 per account per month (average); case price USD 100 per jerky case (some cases over $100; about $30 profit) Markup 30-45%, so the example $1,000 order yields about $350-400 gross profit. Net margin about 30% when owner-operated, about 20% if staff run it. Sells by the case only.
- **Acquisition:** door_to_door_in_person: Walks into independent stores with the product in the truck or trailer, a price sheet and a fun one-pager, and offers to fetch the display on the spot. Re-visits non-buyers every other Tuesday; the second follow-up is usually when they say yes. Early close rate about 4 in 10.; referrals_word_of_mouth: Relationship-based; owners help other local businesses. Existing accounts and regional chains bring more stores (e.g. a 30-store owner, a 25-store regional account, a four-store chain asking him to bring in a brand).; partnerships: Manufacturers want distributors; one jerky brand sent a salesman to open stores for him. Called competitor owners for market insight and brand intros. Used Google to find cool local or non-saturated brands and pitched them using a list of about 50 independent stores.; google_business_profile_maps: Used Google Maps and satellite or street view to find stores that look independent, ignoring fuel branding like Shell or BP, and built the route list from that.
- **Delivery:** Owner drives a pickup with an enclosed trailer (now a box truck) 10-12 stops a day, about 6-8 hours, in a radius of about 2.5 hours around Billings. He is salesperson, driver, merchandiser and order-taker. Monday is warehouse day: pallets are received by staff at a local storage facility and the family helps put away stock. Invoices are made in a cheap app and printed. Buy-backs are rare because he picks products himself.
- **Unit economics (claims):** About $50k/month revenue from about 95 accounts at roughly $500/month each; Gross margin about 35-40%, net margin about 30% owner-operated; Warehouse about $1,400/month for 1,500 sq ft; earlier two storage units about $500/month; One account lost in about 16 months; Full route could gross about $800k/yr with a $50-70k/yr driver
- **Tools:** Cheap invoicing app ($10-12/month), Google / Google Maps, Local storage-facility warehouse with staff receiving, Enclosed trailer / box truck, Manufacturer-supplied racks and displays
- **Startup cost:** Very low: a first manufacturer order of about $2,500 on 14-day terms (free racks), a car or trailer, and a cheap invoice app. Claimed it can be started with almost no money.
- **Time to first revenue:** About $20,000 in sales in the first month in Billings (claimed, per host)
- **UK applicability [E]:** 3/5 — Independent convenience stores, farm shops, garages and pubs exist in the UK, and cash-and-carry or van-sales models are established. The UK is much denser and smaller, so competition is higher and the large-radius rural advantage is less relevant. Food labelling, hygiene registration and shelf-life rules apply. Selling to stores is B2B, so PECR is not a barrier to visits, but cold email or call to sole traders needs care. Prices would be in GBP with local British brands.
- **Risks:** Competing wholesaler can undercut and steal an account (happened once); Perishable or dated goods cause write-offs and buy-backs; Relationships are fragile and scaling needs staff; Heavy physical driving workload, weather (-20°C) and rural distance; Brands may limit territories or terms; Chain stores are hard to enter
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 0/5 (Door-to-door walk-in sales to store owners is in-person and relationship-based. Agents could help build target lists from Maps and manage follow-up schedules, but the selling is human.); onboarding 1/5 (Setting up a store means a physical display install and a verbal agreement, with no contracts. Account records could be digitised.); delivery 0/5 (Driving, carrying cases, merchandising shelves, receiving pallets and buy-backs are all physical work done every visit.); billing 3/5 (Net-10 invoices from an app could be automated with accounting software and payment reminders. Stores currently pay by cheque or cash on the next visit.); support 1/5 (Support is an owner relationship on the next visit; order changes could be handled by messaging but the model depends on face time.)

### I2. Pastry/danish route distribution (Bone Appetit)

- **Customer:** Gas stations, grocery and convenience stores
- **Problem:** The previous owner was a bad operator who hired badly and wanted passive income.
- **Offer:** Resell Bone Appetit danishes and donuts to stores, service them directly.
- **Business model:** Wholesale resale of packaged pastries to retail stores, then sale of the routes as assets.
- **Pricing:** Resale margin of about $0.40 net per donut. net per donut USD 0.4 per donut net Shelf life and outdated returns were a pain.
- **Acquisition:** door_to_door_in_person: Went to existing stores, introduced himself as the new route operator.
- **Delivery:** Shipped from a California bakery, rented freezer space in Colorado, delivered by van to stores himself and with his wife.
- **Unit economics (claims):** Bought at $15k when it grossed about $100k and netted about $30-40k; Grew to about $800k gross in about two years with about $180k profit; Sold his wife's route with van for $85k and his route for $100k
- **Tools:** Rented freezer space, Delivery van
- **Startup cost:** $15,000 cash purchase
- **Time to first revenue:** Business already operating when bought
- **UK applicability [E]:** 3/5 — Packaged baked goods distribution exists in the UK, but short shelf life, hygiene rules and incumbents like big bakery brands apply.
- **Risks:** Short shelf life causes write-offs; Dependence on the bakery, which could pull the plug; Bought with unreliable numbers
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 0/5 (In-person store selling.); onboarding 1/5 (Physical set-up and verbal agreements.); delivery 0/5 (Driving and stocking.); billing 3/5 (Invoicing could be automated.); support 1/5 (Owner visits.)

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Source products distributors cannot get (local and regional brands)** (product)
1. Call competitors and local producers to learn what sells in the area
2. Google for interesting jerky or candy brands not yet in local distribution
3. Ask if they already have distribution in your area, and drop them if they do
4. Prioritise local brands: big wholesalers ignore them
5. Use a list of about 50 independent stores as proof when pitching brands
> "My number one selling jerky is local out of Billings." — Forest (~26:16)

**No-risk merchandising offer** (sales)
1. Position as an addition to the store's existing wholesaler, not a replacement
2. Offer to place orders, stock and rotate the shelf every other week
3. Promise to buy back unsold items
4. Start with one item then expand into a full section
> "I'm not taking over theirs. I'm just an addition to." — Forest (~24:43)

**Cash-flow structure with positive conversion cycle** (finance)
1. Get manufacturer terms net-14 to net-30
2. Give customers net-10, visiting every 14 days so you collect before the next delivery
3. Plough every dollar of early sales into new accounts and topping up stock
> "most of them I have them on net 10." — Forest (~37:21)

**First account playbook** (sales)
1. Carry stock in the vehicle so you can close on the spot
2. Bring a price sheet and a one-page fun-facts flyer with photos
3. Expect several nos; re-visit on a fixed day every other week
4. Win on the second follow-up
5. Use independent-looking stores found on Google Maps, ignoring fuel brand
> "I kept following up every other Tuesday even if I sold them anything or not." — Forest (~68:15)

**Outsourced receiving at a staffed storage facility** (operations)
1. Call local storage facilities that have on-site employees
2. Have staff receive and check pallet deliveries
3. Pay a little extra to avoid being on site
4. Dedicate Monday to warehouse and restocking
> "All my deliveries are received by someone that works at the place." — Forest (~34:07)

**Commission-based hiring for route salespeople** (hiring)
1. Pay route staff a percentage of sales (e.g. 6%) so they grow accounts
2. Staff also act as driver so display and product are in the truck
> "you just make it commission based." — Forest (~51:15)

## Lessons

- *customer_acquisition*: Prove sales bottoms-up (independent accounts first) before approaching distributors or big buyers.
- *niche_selection*: Win by being different: local or regional products avoid price competition with giants.
- *exit_acquisition*: Buying a distressed small business from a bad operator can be a cheap education and a profitable asset.
- *niche_selection*: Working in an industry as an employee gives you contacts and knowledge to start your own version.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Positive cash conversion (collect in 10 days, pay in 14-30) lets you start with almost no capital.
- *offer_design*: Take the burden off the buyer (ordering, inventory risk, buy-backs) to make the offer a no-brainer.
- *validation_testing*: Business owners tend to help other business owners; call competitors in other regions for insight.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: Learn the work before outsourcing it; the previous owner lost value by hiring out something he did not understand.
- *mindset_execution*: Do it and test small; if a product fails with a store you simply do not repeat it.
- *mindset_execution*: Sell your authentic story even without industry experience.
- *automation_ai*: Physical, sweaty businesses face less AI-driven competition, but they also cap how far agents can automate them.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Do not pitch distributors first with a new product; they will not care until it has proven sales
- Avoid big chains like Walmart/Target and avoid competing with Frito-Lay or Coke on price at the start; focus on independents and local products
- Large wholesalers can steal an account when they see your product selling
- Perishable goods and outdated stock create write-offs, so Forest avoids short-dated items
- Buying a business without real numbers is risky; owners who hire out without learning the operation fail
- Markup and margin are not the same; a 50% markup is a 33% margin
