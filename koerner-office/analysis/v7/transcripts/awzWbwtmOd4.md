## Tactics and playbook steps

**Piggyback on trades with an existing stump problem** (acquisition)
1. List tree companies in the surrounding service area, along with landscape installers, fence installers, and concrete contractors who sometimes encounter stumps.
2. Prioritise businesses that rent a grinder or already use a subcontractor.
3. Contact a small batch at a time so the full lead list is not used at once.
4. Ask whether they grind stumps themselves, subcontract the work, or own a machine.
5. Call businesses that respond and explain how the service could save them time or improve turnaround.
> "The P stands for piggyback. So we want to piggyback off of existing businesses that have a pain point, which are tree companies that don't want to do stuff." — Tyler (~35:31)

**Ask for a review after the job** (acquisition)
1. Introduce yourself to the homeowner as the stump grinding operator, even when a tree company brought you to the job.
2. After finishing, explain that you are growing your own company and ask directly for a review.
3. Make it easy for the customer to submit the review while you are there.
4. Keep a record of completed jobs and customer contact details so past customers can be asked later.
> "Hey, I run a different company. I just started and I'm I'm working really hard to grow my company here in Utah County. Would you please give me a review?" — Tyler (~37:33)

**Make quoting simple with photos and measurements** (sales)
1. For a remote quote, ask the customer to text a photo and stump diameter.
2. Measure the widest point of each stump.
3. State a rough estimate and explain that the confirmed quote depends on the photo and measurements.
4. Offer the next available schedule once the customer accepts.
> "If I can just text you real quick and you can text back a picture of the three stumps and just a diameter measurement. And if you get me that, that's all I need for a full quote." — Tyler (~10:43)

**Price for travel, machine wear, and site conditions** (pricing)
1. Set a minimum charge for a visit.
2. Use the widest stump diameter for per-inch pricing.
3. Account for access, stump condition, equipment depreciation, and any requested chip hauling.
4. Do not treat a short job as costless just because the grinding itself takes a few minutes.
> "You got to be careful cuz you're not just including your own man hours. Like this is a very expensive machine and it depreciates." — Tyler (~5:23)

**Test demand before committing to equipment** (validation_testing)
1. Keep the existing job while testing the local market and doing early work around the work schedule.
2. Rent equipment at first instead of buying a grinder immediately.
3. Use early jobs to learn the service, gather reviews, and see whether contractors keep sending work.
4. Consider buying or financing a grinder after demand is clearer.
> "You didn't buy the machine right away. You didn't hop into, you know, large expenses. You tested it on the side." — Chris Koerner (~32:44)

**Win contractor work through responsiveness** (operations)
1. Answer inbound calls consistently.
2. Quote quickly, including by text when the job is straightforward.
3. Schedule promptly when a tree company needs a stump ground before it can collect payment.
4. Communicate immediately if something goes wrong and explain how it will be fixed.
> "Responsiveness is a huge one. Second one is having the equipment that isn't the rental equipment once you get to that point." — Tyler (~35:06)

**Turn repeat questions into a reusable product** (product)
1. Share visual work examples on social media.
2. Notice repeated questions from people asking how to start.
3. Write the answers into a playbook instead of repeating the same introductory call.
4. Use a community or course for buyers who want more guidance.
> "I was getting tons of DMs like, "How do you do this? How do you do this?" Really basic questions and I got really sick of hopping on every call." — Tyler (~26:43)

## Lessons

- *customer_acquisition*: Start with a customer segment that already has the problem and an existing route to the end customer; contractors gave Tyler early jobs before he had a strong homeowner presence.
