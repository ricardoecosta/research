## Summary

Chris Koerner interviews CJ, a Fort Worth operator of four outdoor ice and water vending machines (Everest, Cooler Ice, Twice the Ice, and a large Ice House America-style unit) with no employees beyond one part-time maintenance tech. CJ explains how he got in via his pool-service business's ice spend, why buying distressed existing machines and locations beats building from scratch, how revenue-share vs flat-rent leases work, and the claimed ~90% gross margins. He also covers sale multiples of 2-3x annual profit, avoiding financing, and low-cost entry from $500-$1,000 for a used small machine. Key takeaway: location and buying out stressed single-machine owners are the main levers, but the business needs physical servicing.

### I1. Outdoor ice and water vending machine network

- **Customer:** Tradespeople (pool, yard, electrical crews), contractors, boaters/weekenders, and general public needing cheap ice and water.
- **Problem:** Gas stations and grocers charge high prices for ice (about $4 for 7 lb at CVS/Walgreens); trades buy ice daily.
- **Offer:** 10 lb ice for $2 and 20 lb for $3.50, plus purified water, available 24/7 from street-front machines.
- **Business model:** Per-sale coin/cash/card vending of bagged ice and water; revenue share or flat lease with landlord.
- **Pricing:** Per-bag/per-gallon vending prices. 10 lb ice USD 2 per 10 lb bag; 20 lb ice USD 3.5 per 20 lb bag Competitors CVS/Walgreens about $4 for 7 lb.
- **Acquisition:** other: Drive-by traffic at high-count locations (30-50k cars/day); parking and trailer access matter. Existing machines bring established clientele (this one since ~2008).; referrals_word_of_mouth: Repeat daily trade customers (pool, yard, electrical crews) who find it cheaper than the gas station.; facebook_groups_communities: Sourcing machines and deals (not customers) by networking with other owners on Facebook and calling numbers printed on machines.
- **Delivery:** Machines make and dispense ice automatically. Operator or a roughly $600-$1,000/month tech visits to clear jams, clean, fix parts, collect coins and cash, and test. Remote reset and free-dispense via phone app.
- **Unit economics (claims):** Bag costs 7-10 cents; ice/water ~10-15 cents; ~90% margin per bag; Best location 2-3k/month typical, up to 4-5k busy season; 30% revenue share; New flat-lease location at $550/month, utilities $500-700/month; Four locations hoped to total 15-20k/month revenue, ~5k/month cost; Payback of 100% of money in 1-3 years
- **Tools:** Machine app for remote control and counters, Data.gov traffic counts, Facebook
- **Startup cost:** $500-$1,000 for a used small machine, up to ~$2,500; large Ice House style units $150k-$500k; delivery of machines a couple hundred dollars; may need $10-30k of repair work.
- **Time to first revenue:** Buying an existing location: immediate (first Saturday $1,000-1,500). Building new: 60-90 days best case, possibly 6-9 months due to permitting.
- **UK applicability [E]:** 2/5 — The model depends on US ice-vending culture, hot Texas climate, car-centric lots and cheap parking; UK demand for bagged ice is lower and seasonal, and councils may require planning or permits. Cheap-ice price gaps against UK supermarkets are likely smaller.
- **Risks:** Break-ins and vandalism (one location down for months after being drilled through); Seasonality; winter months drop to $300-400/month; Financed owners get squeezed by slow months; Used machines may need $10-30k of repair; City permitting and code delays for new builds; Location dependence; big machines are hard to move; Landlord rent increases
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 1/5 (Customers are walk-up traffic driven by location; sourcing locations and deals is manual networking, calling and driving around. Software could help find sites but not close them.); onboarding 4/5 (No customer onboarding; the machines are self-serve.); delivery 1/5 (Physical servicing, cleaning, jam clearing, coin and cash collection and repairs need a human on site; remote resets cover only software faults.); billing 4/5 (Coin, cash and card are collected at the machine; the only manual steps are coin and cash collection and paying the landlord.); support 3/5 (Jams and faults are reported by phone call or text and can often be reset remotely via the app; physical faults need a tech.)

### I2. Buy distressed existing ice machines and locations

- **Customer:** Older or overwhelmed single-machine owners looking to exit.
- **Problem:** Single-machine owners are stressed by breakdowns and want out; new builds face permits and unknown locations.
- **Offer:** Take over a machine with established clientele, often for a fraction of its value; a friend got one for $500 worth about 10-15k.
- **Business model:** Buy machines/locations at a discount, repair, and operate or resell.
- **Pricing:** Negotiated purchase; typical multiple 2-3x annual profit. Friend's distressed machine USD 500 one-off; Example sale at ~100k/year profit USD 300000 one-off One seller asking $400-500k for a ~15k/month location (about 3.25x).
- **Acquisition:** facebook_groups_communities: Network with local owners on Facebook; call or text numbers printed on machines.; other: Manufacturers (Everest, Cooler Ice, Twice the Ice) help find locations or sales.
- **Delivery:** Operator takes over the machine and fixes it.
- **Unit economics (claims):** Sale multiples 2-3x annual profit; Machine bought for $500 worth 10-15k, being resold for 15-20k
- **Tools:** Facebook, Phone calls and texts
- **Startup cost:** Low as $500-$1,000; potentially $10-30k of repair work.
- **Time to first revenue:** Immediate on takeover
- **UK applicability [E]:** 2/5 — Same constraints as the base ice business; UK market for ice machines is much smaller.
- **Risks:** Hidden repair costs; Unknown seller claims; Financing squeeze
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 1/5 (Deal sourcing relies on calling and relationships; cold texting individuals is restricted in the UK under PECR.); onboarding 1/5 (Negotiation and transfer are human-led.); delivery 1/5 (Physical repair and operation.); billing 4/5 (Machine sales are automatic.); support 2/5 (Remote app resets with human on-site backup.)

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Find and buy from stressed single-machine owners** (acquisition)
1. Drive around looking for machines in your area
2. Call or text the owner numbers printed on the machine
3. Look for owners with a single machine who are exhausted by breakdowns
4. Offer to take it over; consider sellers who have let machines go
5. Network with owners on Facebook and ask how they started
> "if you could go to the machines in your area and just call the owners and finally catch one" — CJ (~9:17)

**Pick locations by traffic and access** (operations)
1. Check traffic counts on a .gov site
2. Target 30-50k cars per day
3. Ensure parking and trailer access
4. Use revenue share at very busy sites to share downside

**Hire one roaming tech** (hiring)
1. Pay one person about $600-1,000/month
2. Visit all machines to clear jams and clean
3. Operator remotely resets and dispenses free water from the app

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Turn a cost centre you know well into a business; the operator started because his pool company spent thousands a month on ice.
- *exit_acquisition*: Buying a running asset from a tired owner usually beats building from scratch on price, speed and risk.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Revenue-share leases align landlord incentives and share downside during outages, at the cost of a lower margin.
- *mindset_execution*: Start small with one unit and grow from cash flow rather than debt.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Seasonality matters; winter revenue can fall to a tenth of peak.
- *operations*: Physical servicing is the bottleneck for scaling unattended assets; a single roaming tech lets one owner handle several machines.
- *scaling*: Unglamorous niches with fragmented, ageing local owners offer roll-up opportunities.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Avoid financing machines; slow seasons and repairs squeeze financed owners into selling.
- Buying a machine before securing a location is a gamble; permitting can delay launch up to 6-9 months.
- Machines can be broken into; one location was down for a couple of months.
