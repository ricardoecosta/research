## Tactics and playbook steps

**Match the franchise to the buyer** (niche_selection)
1. Start by asking what the buyer wants from the business, their risk tolerance, operational background, and financial goals.
2. For risk-averse buyers, look for established brands with around 80 to 100 open units and 5 to 10 years or more of history.
3. For buyers seeking greater upside, consider earlier systems with protected territory while recognising that the risk is higher.
> "I start with the individual like what do you want? What's your risk tolerance? What are you good at?" — Alex Smerzneck (~7:00)

**Use franchise financing to reduce initial cash** (finance)
1. Ask lenders about SBA financing for the franchise.
2. Estimate the cash contribution after financing before committing.
3. Alex says friends or family may fund the remaining amount if the buyer lacks it.
> "And if you don't have $10,000, you can get friends or family to finance that." — Chris Koerner (~1:41)

**Check the franchise disclosure document and Item 19 footnotes** (validation_testing)
1. Review the FDD for units sold and closed, closure reasons, lawsuits, litigation, and bankruptcies.
2. Read Item 19 financial disclosures and inspect footnotes for adjusted EBITDA or revenue.
3. Compare the stated earnings with franchisee costs and operating requirements before deciding.
> "Look at the footnotes and make sure you're you're getting the whole whole set of information." — Alex Smerzneck (~32:58)

**Build a referral engine for home-sale preparation** (acquisition)
1. Use the franchisor's referral playbooks to approach real-estate agents and brokerages.
2. Build repeat relationships with agents who handle multiple home sales.
3. Create a dependable bench of electricians, plumbers, drywall workers, and other subcontractors.
4. Offer an option to pay at closing if the brand supports it.
> "And if you get a high-volume realtor on your team, like that could be dozens of closings a year." — Chris Koerner (~27:35)

**Use routes and subscriptions for window-box services** (operations)
1. Sell installation per window as the initial project.
2. Offer different flower replacement frequencies and seasonal add-ons.
3. Schedule customers into regular routes to replace flowers and decorations.
> "And then there's this recurring subscription, and they have, as you can imagine, all sorts of add-ons." — Alex Smerzneck (~29:34)

**Use franchise lead tools to find short-term rental cleaning clients** (acquisition)
1. Target Airbnb owners and operators as a cleaning niche.
2. Use franchise-provided lists and lead tools where available.
3. Contact prospects only through channels permitted by the applicable marketing and privacy rules.
> "they have those tools to go scrape all the Airbnb owners and operators and, you know, drive you business." — Alex Smerzneck (~37:09)

**Scale a franchise portfolio around operating strengths** (scaling)
1. Focus on raising or borrowing capital, selecting sites, opening locations, and operating teams.
2. Use the franchisor's systems for branding, supply chain, and menu innovation.
3. Repeat the process across additional units and, potentially, different brands.
> "I go raise capital or I borrow capital and I repeat that." — Alex Smerzneck (~39:08)

**Use AI to review an FDD** (validation_testing)
1. Upload the FDD to Claude or ChatGPT.
2. Ask for red flags, yellow flags, and green flags.
3. Verify the resulting analysis against the source document and its footnotes.
> "upload it to Claude or ChatGPT and be like, all right, cut cut through the noise. Like, what are the red flags, yellow flags, green flags here?" — Chris Koerner (~33:14)

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Choose a business that matches the buyer's risk tolerance, strengths, and financial objective rather than selecting solely by headline revenue.
- *validation_testing*: A larger number of established franchise units and years in operation can indicate that a playbook has been tested, but do not replace due diligence.
- *offer_design*: A franchise's value can come from its brand, leads, technology, relationships, and operating playbook, not just the product or service.
- *operations*: A franchise owner still needs to run operations; franchisor support does not make a physical service business passive.
- *pricing*: Recurring revenue and add-ons can raise customer value beyond the initial installation.
- *customer_acquisition*: Referral partners can become a repeatable acquisition engine when they encounter many potential customers and share incentives.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Review the whole FDD, including notes and adjustments, before trusting revenue or margin claims.
- *scaling*: A multi-unit operator can benefit from brand systems and focus on site selection, capital, and team operations.
- *validation_testing*: A franchise may offer a trade-off between lower independent startup costs and the support, credibility, and relationships of an established system.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Alex says many brand-new franchise concepts fail; a founder who has franchised a business once is not proof that the system is durable.
- Do not rely only on headline revenue or adjusted earnings. Alex says to inspect FDD footnotes and the full set of disclosures.
- Home-improvement returns are based on estimates; the seller cannot know exactly what the house would have sold for without the work.
- The flower-box service may be more exposed to a recession because it is a discretionary service for affluent households.
- Franchise revenue and profit claims in the conversation are not independently verified here and may describe different operators or territories.
- Cleaning lead generation based on scraped Airbnb-owner lists and cold outreach could raise privacy and marketing-law issues; UK electronic marketing rules must be followed.
- The franchise model still requires an operator to manage people, physical work, sites, and service quality; it is not presented as unattended.
