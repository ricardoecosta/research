## Tactics and playbook steps

**Model the business before scaling sales** (finance)
1. Ask: if sales doubled tomorrow, which departments break (purchasing, customer service, production)?
2. Financially model margin, equipment, receivables, inventory and cash position at the higher volume
3. Decide capital allocation (new building, second shift) before hiring salespeople
4. Commit to the plan so early missteps do not cause you to back off
> "you need to have a plan for all of that before you get too excited about investing in the sales sales piece" — David Heacock (~29:48)

**Master target map with tracked touch points** (acquisition)
1. Pick industry segments (hospitals, janitorial, industrial, food service)
2. Build a master list of every distributor in each segment
3. Assign a specialist rep per segment
4. Track calls, emails, trade show meetings per target per quarter in the CRM
5. Analyse which segments give the most gross profit and pay on time, then staff up there
> "you need to figure out how you're going to track how many touch points each of those each of those targets have with you" — David Heacock (~42:06)

**Delivered pricing without surcharges** (pricing)
1. Quote all prices delivered to the customer's location
2. Do not show fuel/freight surcharges on invoices (customers expect them to go away)
3. Keep the margin when freight falls to offset unpassed material increases
> "You put that on an invoice that there's a freight surcharge, they expect it to go away at some point" — Jake (~16:48)

**Regional direct-to-end-user delivery** (operations)
1. Target end users (hotels, gyms) within about 200 miles of the factory
2. Truck product daily to a same-day courier sort centre (e.g. Roadie) outside the city
3. Get it there by 2pm for same-day van delivery
4. Hire distributor sales reps who already have the relationships
> "if you get product there by um 2:00 p.m., it gets delivered same day" — David Heacock (~50:55)

**Same page exercise for family businesses** (mindset)
1. Bring in a high-EQ consultant
2. Each family member answers the same ~10 questions on paper separately (revenue target, exit amount, timing)
3. Read answers to each other to expose misalignment
4. Then plan how to engineer the outcome the senior generation wants (buyouts, partners)
> "It's called the same page exercise" — Jake (~22:05)

**Touch product as few times as possible** (operations)
1. Stack filters off the production line; box only when shipping to the end customer
2. Use multiple distribution points to reach most customers next day

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Boring, unchanging products (toilet paper, filters) offer stability because demand will exist in 50 years regardless of AI.
- *offer_design*: Choose products customers use up and rebuy so you are not hunting for a new customer each sale.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Revenue alone does not make you more money; business model and quality of revenue do.
- *offer_design*: Middlemen face compressed margins; owning the end-customer relationship compounds.
- *operations*: In freight-heavy low-margin products, business density clusters near the factory.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Segment customers by profitability and payment behaviour; you could fire 10% of customers and make more money.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: In family businesses, split responsibilities cleanly and define final decision rights.
- *mindset_execution*: Get clarity on the outcome you want, then reverse engineer the business plan; keep refining it like polishing a marble.
- *customer_acquisition*: Partner with channels that have a genuine advantage (e.g. Walmart pickup) and serve directly where they do not.
- *legal_risk*: Do estate planning early to minimise tax and control problems at succession.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Selling a business to buyers who do not understand it can see it run into the ground; knowing your business does not mean you can run someone else's.
- Do not start with manufacturing; it needs capital and the sales process is the harder part.
- In distribution, growth consumes cash as receivables and inventory grow, which can force you to back off.
- Fast sales growth overloaded back-office departments and caused mistakes.
- Price increases on a commodity product risk losing business and create heavy admin and negotiation work.
- Avoiding tough conversations about expectations in a family business sets everyone up for misery.
- Leaving estate planning until later is costlier and more painful, especially before a change of control.
