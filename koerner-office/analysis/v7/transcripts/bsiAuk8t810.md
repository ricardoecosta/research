## Tactics and playbook steps

**Repackage a proven product through a new form factor** (product)
1. Start with a product category that people already buy.
2. Identify a format that is inconvenient, unpleasant, or hard to carry.
3. Change the format while keeping the familiar underlying use case, such as putting greens in a gummy.
4. Use direct-response demonstrations to explain the new format.
> "take something that's already really popular and just change the form factor." — Connor (~4:28)

**Sell a visible solution to an embarrassment problem** (offer_design)
1. Identify a problem customers feel in public, such as a stain on clothing or a visible pimple.
2. Design the offer around making the problem easier to face, even if it does not solve the root cause.
3. Make the solution easy to carry and use in the moment.
4. Consider consumable packs to create repeat purchases.
> "You're solving embarrassment. the parent kid doesn't care, right? If I'm out with my kids and they get a big stain on their shirt" — Connor (~7:58)

**Use retail buyer relationships to expand a product line** (sales)
1. Build a relationship with a category buyer at a retailer.
2. Ask which products in the category sell well and what customers want next.
3. Use the buyer's answer to develop a differentiated product.
4. Return with the new product and use the existing relationship to seek additional shelf space.
> "then that's your channel right and so now it's it is let me sell as much as possible and make sure that the product is good but now let me get more products to also sell to you" — Connor (~10:33)

**Test event distribution by seeding race organisers** (customer_acquisition)
1. Find a race with a known number of participant spots.
2. Send the race director enough sample tattoos for the field.
3. Offer them to include the samples in participant bags.
4. Ask for feedback and follow-up orders if participants like the product.
> "I would just proactively send the race director, you know, if I know they're they have 3,000 spots, I'll send them 3,000 tattoos" — Chris Koerner (~20:09)

**Sell measurable sponsor exposure on an event product** (sales)
1. Estimate the event audience and the number of items distributed.
2. Use an adoption estimate to describe potential sponsor exposure.
3. Offer sponsor placement on the tattoo and consider a QR code or participant upsell.
4. Explore whether the brand, the event, or both will pay.
> "You could get Gatorade to pay for it. You could get the race to pay for it." — Chris Koerner (~21:03)

**Put custom options behind a simple product form** (product)
1. Ask customers which outcomes they care about.
2. Map their answers to a manageable number of pre-made combinations.
3. Make the customer-facing choice feel custom while keeping manufacturing options limited.
4. Validate manufacturing feasibility before promising the offer.
> "And maybe like on the back end there's like 20 different combinations. That way there's not like two million combinations" — Chris Koerner (~13:48)

**Build trust by removing affiliate conflicts** (offer_design)
1. Choose a narrow category where buyers struggle to verify quality.
2. Test products with an independent third party.
3. Charge users for access rather than relying on affiliate links.
4. Show testing status and make untested products clearly distinguishable.

## Lessons

- *offer_design*: Look for a familiar product that is awkward to use, carry, or consume, then test whether a different form factor makes it easier to adopt.
- *offer_design*: A product can succeed by addressing the moment a customer feels embarrassed, even if it does not solve the underlying cause.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Repeat purchases are easier to build when the product is consumable and fits into an existing habit.
- *customer_acquisition*: Direct-response product marketing still uses familiar techniques: strong visual hooks and demonstrations that make the benefit easy to understand.
- *customer_acquisition*: Retail relationships can reveal what buyers want next and create an opening to pitch differentiated products.
- *operations*: Packaging can be part of the product innovation, but a novel packaging format may require manufacturers to change their process.
- *validation_testing*: Physical-world concepts often hinge on manufacturing, distribution, or event partnerships, so validate those dependencies early.
- *offer_design*: Trust can be a product feature when buyers cannot easily verify quality; independent testing and avoiding affiliate conflicts are possible ways to support it.
- *legal_risk*: In supplements and health products, efficacy matters because customers are buying an intended outcome, not just taste or convenience.
- *validation_testing*: Do not confuse a plausible idea or a large market with proof of demand; the speakers repeatedly leave pricing, adoption, and market size open questions.
- *mindset_execution*: Optimisation can become counterproductive when small measurements dominate how someone feels about their health.
- *offer_design*: Make a lengthy experience more accessible by testing a shorter version that fits into a normal day.
