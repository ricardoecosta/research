## Tactics and playbook steps

**Practice before taking a paid job** (validation_testing)
1. Find an old parking lot and practice following existing lines with water in the machine.
2. Do a very small free job for a nonprofit to learn how the work feels and build confidence.
3. Track material use and adjust technique before quoting larger sites.
> "I went down to just an old parking lot. Here's the machine, I put some water in it and I just tried to run follow along the lines" — Hunter (~1:15)

**Use a local Facebook post to create a first proof job** (acquisition)
1. Post in the town's Facebook page and ask residents to nominate a business for a free refresh.
2. Use the free job to get local attention and show the result.
3. Contact the nominated businesses to ask whether they want paid quotes.
> "I wanted to reach out to all of them to see if they would pay after. Yeah. For you know, we'll we'll choose the winner" — Hunter (~2:59)

**Build an email prospect list and follow up** (acquisition)
1. Buy a targeted local list from a list provider found on Upwork; Hunter paid $5 to $6 for a list.
2. Email prospects with a simple introduction, a good-price offer, and a specific seasonal reason to act.
3. Start with a manageable batch, track replies and quotes, then follow up rather than endlessly adding new prospects.
4. Filter UK contacts so unsolicited marketing email is sent only where PECR permits.
> "And so my close rate is better in person but it is what it is. What is your close rate? Um it's like 40 to 50%." — Hunter (~25:39)

**Quote in person when practical** (sales)
1. Visit the site and count lines, stencils, arrows, no-parking areas, and difficult sections.
2. Ask whether old lines need to be redrawn or realigned.
3. Send a written quote with Quote IQ; Hunter said some customers do not need an in-person meeting.
4. Price travel and difficult visibility or layout work higher.
> "Some of them just want to they don't want to meet me. They they don't need to. Okay. Yeah. Yeah and so my close rate is better in person" — Hunter (~25:33)

**Start with a roller and buy a machine after demand is proven** (finance_unit_economics)
1. If capital is tight, start with a roller, paint, and an email address.
2. Expect more taping and slower delivery than with a striping machine.
3. Use early job revenue to buy a machine once demand and job size justify it.
> "If you don't have the money for a machine, you start with this. It's tedious, but it's cheap. It's 20 bucks." — Hunter (~30:08)

**Upsell requested services and learn the work when subcontractors are unavailable** (offer_design)
1. Ask customers who requested striping whether they also want seal-coating or crack-filling quotes.
2. Price each service separately and keep the scope clear.
3. If subcontractors are too busy or unreliable, decide whether to buy equipment and learn the service directly.
> "I wanted to sub out the seal coating and I would talk to these other companies and they would say, "Yeah, I could do it in a month."" — Hunter (~22:48)

**Protect cash flow on material-heavy jobs** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Take a 50% deposit for seal-coating work because material costs are higher.
2. Agree when the remaining balance is due before starting.
3. For lower-cost striping jobs, Hunter said he was comfortable collecting payment at the end.
> "For seal coating, the material costs a lot more. And so we'll charge 50% up up front." — Hunter (~14:58)

**Estimate crack-fill jobs with depth risk in mind** (pricing)
1. Use a measuring wheel for simpler cracks and quote per linear foot.
2. For dense lots, estimate the overall job rather than measuring every crack.
3. Check depth and likely material use; visible width alone can understate the amount of fill needed.

**Use visible job-site presentation to earn nearby work** (acquisition)
1. Block off the active area with cones or barricades so vehicles do not drive through fresh work.
2. Put the company name on signs or barricades so nearby businesses can see who did the job.
3. Use respectful communication and professional behavior even if the vehicle is not impressive.
> "And so it's it's helpful just to keep the lot blocked off but then also to just say, "Hey, this looks good. We're the ones who did it." — Hunter (~34:47)

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: A small, low-cost job can provide a practical test of the work and create local proof before committing to more equipment.
- *customer_acquisition*: A simple local post can create attention and give a new operator a reason to approach several nearby prospects.
- *pricing*: Price the actual difficulty of the site: travel, faint lines, stencils, and realignment can make a job more involved than counting standard lines.
- *customer_acquisition*: Follow up on quotes and allow for slow approval cycles; a full pipeline can turn into revenue later even when the first month is modest.
- *scaling*: When customers repeatedly request adjacent work, consider adding the service if subcontractors cannot meet demand or quality needs.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Do not price crack filling from appearance alone; depth can change material use and erase the margin.
- *other*: Professionalism is shown through respectful behavior and reliable work, although Hunter said his close rate improved after he got a truck.
- *mindset_execution*: Starting before feeling fully confident can lead to larger opportunities; Hunter quoted a $26,000 job despite arriving in a minivan.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Hunter said a crack-filling job with cracks six or seven inches deep consumed enough material that he worked for two days and walked away with no profit.
- Used striping equipment was cheaper but required Hunter to replace some parts and learn repairs.
- Church customers may need board approval, which Hunter said can take a couple of weeks.
- Seal coating and striping work is exposed to weather; Hunter described rushing to complete jobs before colder conditions arrived.
- For asphalt Hunter recommended water-based paint; he said oil-based paint on a seal-coated surface can turn yellow and hazy.
- Hunter said he initially tried door knocking but found it was not the best use of his time.
- The host's sponsor segment makes claims about LLC liability protection and tax write-offs; those are promotional claims, not independently established in the interview.
- The host promoted a paid business community, and Hunter promoted online guides and in-person training, creating promotional conflicts around business advice.
- For UK outreach, do not copy the US-style email tactic without checking PECR: unsolicited marketing email to individuals and sole traders generally requires consent, while corporate subscribers may be contacted subject to applicable rules.
