## Summary

David Heacock interviews Justin, who was fired from TGI Fridays and later from a commercial floor care firm, and now runs Midland Floor Care, a commercial floor care business in the DC area that did close to $450k last year. Justin started in residential cleaning with rented Home Depot equipment and bought leads, then moved into commercial work after asking a Massage Envy owner who did her floors. He grew through franchise referrals, and later through government contracts and bidding to prime contractors. The coaching half concludes his limiting factor is sales. David recommends hiring one proven salesperson from competitors or adjacent markets on a commission-heavy plan, building the sales financial model first, and incentivising contractors as partners.

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Ask every business you visit who does their floors** (acquisition)
1. Look at the floor wherever you go
2. Ask staff/owner 'who is doing your floors?'
3. Put the first client on a recurring program and follow them as they expand to more franchise units
4. Get referred within the franchise community
> "Yo, who is doing your floors? Y'all this I can, you know, y'all need some help" — Justin (~15:53)

**Prime contractor bidding to diversify** (acquisition)
1. Sign up on PlanHub and BuildingConnected
2. Bid on floor finishing/polishing packages
3. Collect GC contact info and call: anything else coming, are we in line as a vendor, what certifications do you need
> "they have different like websites like planhub and building connected and all of that where signed up all all on those and um just bidding on contracts" — Justin (~23:54)

**Use temp labour instead of fixed headcount** (operations)
1. Keep 3-4 core staff
2. Staff up per project via temp agencies so idle salaries aren't carried

**Hire a first salesperson from competitors** (hiring)
1. Network ~25% of your time to find someone already selling to your target customer
2. Look at competitors, adjacent markets (other cities) and adjacent products (flooring distributors)
3. Offer higher commission or bigger piece of the action
4. Commission-heavy structure, budget ~6 months of draw while they ramp
5. Do 90% of sales calls with them for the first 6 months
6. Or use independent reps on commission only
> "I would find somebody that's working for one of your competitors and give them a opportunity to make more money" — David Heacock (~42:36)

**Build the sales financial model before hiring** (sales)
1. Define pricing rules
2. Define commission structure aligned to margin (avoid reps underpricing)
3. Know delivery cost and margin
4. Set pipeline metrics: leads per week, follow-ups

**Bootstrap equipment** (finance)
1. Do temp work for cash
2. Rent equipment from Home Depot for first jobs
3. Buy equipment from profits

## Lessons

- *mindset_execution*: Working inside an industry first, then starting your own firm in it, is a strong path.
- *customer_acquisition*: Being personable in customers' homes is rare and wins repeat business.
- *niche_selection*: Target the underserved mid-size segment big competitors ignore.
- *mindset_execution*: Small owners can't afford the best people, so they must be best at sales themselves until they can.
- *scaling*: Identify the limiting factor (here sales, not delivery) and take the highest-leverage action on it.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: Prove a sales hire works once before scaling the sales team.
- *mindset_execution*: The answer is in doing, not in another course or podcast.
- *customer_acquisition*: Turn adjacent players (contractors, reps) into incentivised partners to multiply your sales force.
- *operations*: Variable temp labour keeps risk low in project-based service work.
- *customer_acquisition*: A performance-focused website can turn a near-dead inbound channel into steady calls.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Big-name commercial clients may come with net 60-120 terms that strain cash flow.
- Having most revenue tied to government contracts is concentration risk.
- Being personable is not the same as being good at sales; owners avoid asking for the sale.
- Cutting residual commissions drove away good salespeople at his former employer.
- Commission reps may price too low to earn commission while the business loses money.
- Hiring someone just 'supposedly good at sales' is a mistake; hire someone who has sold to your customer.
