## Summary

UpFlip visits Get Lost Shaved Ice in Virginia Beach, a Hawaiian shave ice shop opened in October 2023 by Stephanie and her husband Todd after a Hawaii honeymoon. They spent eight to nine months on recipe R&D, make about 10 dye-free syrups from scratch every Monday, sell $9.50-13.50 bowls with an estimated goods cost of cents per syrup portion, and run a food truck in summer (about 15% of revenue). 2025 revenue was $408.5k with a best month of about $95k, but after labour ($114k), rent ($50k+), $65k owner pay and other costs, net was about $23.5k; merch is claimed at about 20% of revenue at 50-60% margins. Lessons: the business is extremely seasonal and labour-heavy (3-4 staff in winter, 25 in summer), the buildout doubled from $60k, health department approval was hard, and a non-literal brand name enabled merch.

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Free product for local micro-creators** (acquisition)
1. Find content creators with concentrated local followings
2. Invite them for free product rather than paying
3. Let first-time reactions ('life changing') drive word of mouth
> "finding content creators within your field" — Stephanie (~18:58)

**Pick a brand name that sells merch** (product)
1. Choose a name unrelated to the product (Get Lost) that people want to wear
2. Sell sweatshirts, tees, tanks for kids and adults in an on-site merch shed
3. Merch earns 50-60% margin with no labour per unit
> "No one wants to wear Virginia Beach Shave Ice or a T-shirt, right" — Todd (~22:16)

**Start simple, then expand the menu** (product)
1. Launch with a few flavours you do really well
2. Add flavours and monthly specials once quality is proven
3. Run local collaboration specials
> "find what you can do and do it really, really well to start off. And then start adding" — Stephanie (~18:07)

**Buffer buildout budgets** (finance)
1. Add at least 10-15% to buildout estimates
2. Expect worse; theirs doubled
> "you have to like add 10 to 15% on a build-out" — Stephanie (~7:03)

**Pass card fees to customers** (pricing)
1. Note merchant fees (~3%, $14k/year)
2. Add a surcharge to sales to recoup them

**Research regulations before buying a truck** (legal)
1. Check local food truck rules (e.g. time limits in one spot)
2. Check health department and zoning
3. Use AI tools to list the red tape for your area
> "I would definitely recommend if someone was thinking about it to look into the regulations before purchasing a food truck" — Stephanie (~9:15)

**Dual-use spaces for seasonality** (operations)
1. Use a shed as heated/cooled winter lounge seating
2. Flip it to a merch shop in summer
3. Open only Fri-Sun in winter

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Bring a product you discover while travelling to a market that doesn't have it.
- *offer_design*: Quality, scratch-made ingredients and branding justify premium prices.
- *finance_unit_economics*: High-margin merch can subsidise a labour-heavy core product.
- *validation_testing*: Test cheaply (cart or truck) before committing to a brick-and-mortar buildout.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Revenue is not profit; seasonal food businesses can have high sales and thin margins.
- *mindset_execution*: Flexibility and calm problem solving are core owner skills; something breaks every day.
- *niche_selection*: Year-round warm markets (Florida) remove the seasonality problem for weather-dependent products.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Keeping ownership (no investors) means self-funding risk.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Food trucks face location time limits; a fixed truck with seating was not allowed.
- Health inspectors resisted a new concept (making own ice blocks and syrups); expect many 'no's.
- Seasonality is extreme: sales drop the day kids return to school, winters are dismal.
- Despite $408k revenue, net was only ~$23.5k; labour is the biggest line item.
- Seasonal staff mostly don't return, so you retrain every year.
- Using life savings and personal loans was terrifying; debt still being repaid.
