## Summary

Chris Koerner interviews Mark, a 66-year-old lawyer who designs 'business in a box' ideas, about his nitro iced coffee cart built from a gutted Home Depot tool cart for under ~$1-3k. Using shelf-stable brewed coffee in bag-in-box packs and a nitro tap, he pours 24oz iced coffees in 30 seconds for ~$3 cost and ~$8.50 price, netting $400 at small events, $800-1,200 at festivals and $8,000 at his best two-day event. He also ran beer tap trucks and armoire tap bar rentals, and sets up operators (divorced moms, veterans, visa immigrants) on a ~30% profit split. Key takeaway: strip a product to what customers actually buy, keep capex tiny, and win on location.

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Strip the coffee cart down to what customers buy** (product)
1. Observe that after 9am most drinks are iced with syrup/cream
2. Skip $30k espresso machines; buy brewed coffee from a local brewer
3. Switch from heavy kegs to bag-in-box with a gas-driven pump
4. Limit menu to iced coffee and latte with a few syrups
5. Pour in 30 seconds
> "the second you put that cream and that syrup in there, the quality of the underlying coffee doesn't matter" — Mark (~17:19)

**Event pitch selection and placement** (operations)
1. Arrive early to watch set-up and traffic flow
2. Prefer the food area over isolated merchandise areas
3. Pick events without competing options (air shows, remote festivals)
4. Calibrate: bad days usually mean wrong location, date or season
> "I believe that I'm better off in the food area" — Mark (~44:17)

**Cheap, testable signage** (acquisition)
1. Design signs on your computer
2. Print on PVC at FedEx Office for ~$100
3. Iterate wording; simple 'ICE COFFEE' outperformed 'nitro' and 'latte'
> "I now go back to just ice coffee. That's it" — Mark (~43:00)

**Become the organiser's helper to get into events** (acquisition)
1. Support a new event organiser early (lend trucks, fill gaps)
2. Earn favourable access and fees as they grow

**Card-only revenue split for operators** (finance)
1. Set operators up on your Stripe or Square account
2. Revenue lands in your account
3. Split ~30% to you depending on who supplies what
> "They use my Stripe account, a Stripe account or a square account" — Mark (~55:41)

**Side-door venue access** (acquisition)
1. Meet gas station/car wash owners on LinkedIn or at conferences
2. Pitch a story (e.g. a single mum operating 10-3) rather than asking about fees
3. Target car washes, gas stations, semi-pro sports, food courts

## Lessons

- *offer_design*: Sell what customers actually buy, not what the founder is romantic about.
- *validation_testing*: Lower the cost of being wrong: a cheap, non-perishable setup makes testing locations cheap.
- *niche_selection*: Cluster with competitors: food courts and auto rows beat being the unique option.
- *operations*: A simple menu speeds service and increases throughput.
- *operations*: Design for human factors so more kinds of operators can run it.
- *exit_acquisition*: Young founders with a working concept often give away territories cheaply; strike deals early.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Watch bankrupt businesses: owners who don't cut costs early go down.
- *customer_acquisition*: Approach gatekeepers through the side door with a story, not a cold ask about fees.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Expensive equipment-heavy setups ($30k espresso machines, $50k trucks) kill returns and break down.
- Alcohol events bring liability worries (over-serving, underage drinking verdicts).
- Wrong location, date or season can produce $100 days.
- Operating without permits ('cowboying') is risky; public street vending is most regulated. UK note: street trading consents and food registration required.
- Founders romanticise product aspects customers don't care about.
- Heavy kegs are a human-factors problem (injuries) and exclude many operators.
