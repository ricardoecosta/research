## Tactics and playbook steps

**Enter through an experienced inspector and learn on real jobs** (hiring_outsourcing)
1. Find an experienced draw inspector willing to mentor you.
2. Go to several inspections with the mentor and learn what the lender's checklist asks you to document.
3. Use a basic understanding of construction phases and the checklist; Lou says the work is learnable without having personally built a house.
4. Build skill by documenting the location, progress, and missing items accurately.
> "The easiest way is to get an inspector to kind of mentor you and kind of help you along." — Lou (~7:36)

**Join inspection platforms and price competitively** (acquisition)
1. Sign up with inspection companies and marketplaces that route lender requests.
2. Review the offered location and fee before accepting a job.
3. Set a competitive rate when the platform lets you choose; Lou says a high price can send the work to a cheaper inspector.
4. Build a record of timeliness and thorough reports so lenders and inspection companies continue to use you.
> "I priced myself I think competitively 'cause you kind of set your price. How much you'll do it for." — Lou (~9:30)

**Respond quickly and keep the day flexible** (operations)
1. Accept or decline new platform requests promptly.
2. Call the builder or homeowner to arrange access and give a near-term visit window.
3. Avoid pinning the whole day to fixed appointment times because new jobs may arrive.
4. Submit the report within the inspection company's requested 24–48-hour window.
> "I try not to pin myself to a time because my days will change in the drop of a hat." — Lou (~11:39)

**Plan routes and group nearby jobs** (operations)
1. The night before, enter the next day's addresses in Google Maps.
2. Order stops to reduce backtracking and driving time.
3. When additional jobs arrive in the same area, add them to the route if feasible.
4. Recheck travel distance and the offered fee before taking a distant job.
> "I'll sit down usually night before and I'll map out the next day's work. I'll get out of Google Maps, I'll plug in where I'm going and I'll look at 'em and then I'll strategically put them in order." — Lou (~12:17)

**Make the inspection evidence easy for the lender to verify** (operations)
1. Use parcel data to confirm the property when house numbers or job boxes are missing.
2. Take a screenshot of the parcel and use it as the first photo in the submission.
3. Keep phone location settings enabled so photos include location data.
4. Tell the story of progress with useful photos, including items that are not installed yet.
5. Use a drone when terrain makes ground-level exterior photos difficult.
> "I take pictures of things that are there and this is gonna sound crazy but I take pictures of the things that are not there." — Lou (~14:03)

**Complete and upload reports on site when connectivity allows** (operations)
1. Use the platform app or website required by the company that ordered the inspection.
2. Upload photos and complete the draw sheet after the visit.
3. Lou added Starlink to submit from the field where cell signal is weak, avoiding an hour of evening paperwork at home.
> "Buying the Starlink was probably one of the best things I've done. That allowed me to do the inspection on site and submit it." — Lou (~30:47)

**Find short-term rental clients through local relationships** (acquisition)
1. Post in relevant local Facebook pages asking whether owners need property checks.
2. Contact a local real-estate project manager who manages short-term rentals.
3. Offer a recurring condition report to owners who live far away.
4. Price large properties based on the actual time and checklist scope; Lou says he underpriced his first large house.
> "I also reached out to a local project manager with a real estate company and said, "You know, she managed all their short-term rentals."" — Lou (~23:40)

**Keep draw inspections within the lender's scope** (legal)
1. Report what is installed and what is not installed; do not give credit for materials merely waiting on site.
2. Avoid taking sides in disputes between homeowners and builders.
3. Do not present a draw inspection as a judgment on workmanship when the task is to document progress for the lender.
> "We can only give credit for things that are actually installed. So it's gotta be screwed in, set in, set in place." — Lou (~43:40)

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: A narrow service can work without deep prior trade experience when the client supplies a checklist and an experienced operator can teach the process.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: A mentor can shorten the path to a first job by showing a newcomer what to observe and how to report it.
- *finance_unit_economics*: For local field work, route planning and geographic density matter because driving can take more time than the task itself.
- *customer_acquisition*: Build reliability through prompt, thorough work; repeat platform and lender work depends on being timely and dependable.
- *operations*: Document absences as well as completed work when the customer needs an accurate view of progress.
- *pricing*: Price a service around its full scope and time, especially when a property or job is larger than expected.
- *legal_risk*: Stay in the role the customer hired you for and avoid taking responsibility for disputes or judgments outside that scope.
- *mindset_execution*: Keep side-hustle capacity aligned with the life you want; Lou chose not to push for full-time growth after becoming busy.
- *mindset_execution*: Act on a promising idea while motivation is present instead of waiting indefinitely.
- *scaling*: A repeatable service can create room for adjacent income streams, such as adding property checks or teaching the process to others.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Construction sites can be hazardous. Lou warns about fresh concrete, newly finished floors, elevated decks without handrails, and getting locked behind a door without an exterior handle.
- Do not walk on fresh or protected finishes if doing so could damage them; Lou says he stayed off a floor with a sign warning people not to walk on it.
- Stay within the lender's task. Lou avoids adjudicating builder disputes or commenting on workmanship beyond documenting whether checklist items are installed.
- Do not give credit for items that are merely on site; Lou says they must actually be installed.
- Short-term rental checks can expose the inspector to liability from guest belongings left in drawers or under beds, including firearms.
- Lou says he underpriced his first short-term rental maintenance job, a large seven-bedroom house that took more work than he expected.
- Construction activity and geography constrain demand; the work may not suit an area with little construction, and Lou says driving is a major expense.
- Lou says he should consider errors and omissions insurance in addition to his liability policy.
- Electronic outreach to individuals and sole traders in the UK is restricted by PECR; the Facebook group tactic described should not be extended into unsolicited messages without consent.
