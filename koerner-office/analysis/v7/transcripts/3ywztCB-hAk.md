## Summary

Chris Koerner interviews Rex Lindsay, owner of Cozy Pros, a Phoenix home-performance business selling attic insulation removal/re-insulation, Aeroseal duct sealing, duct cleaning and air scrubbers, mostly via Facebook ads offering a free attic inspection. Rex says he moved from ~100 in-house employees to subcontracting nearly all installation work, with a project manager on site, citing lower insurance, equipment and quality-risk burden. He gives average ticket (~$10k), margin structure (~52% gross, 15-20% net target), lead cost (~$75) and ad return claims. Key takeaway: learn lead generation and sales, subcontract the labor, and use cheap front-end offers (duct cleaning, free inspection) to upsell large tickets.

### I1. Subcontracted attic insulation + Aeroseal duct sealing package (Cozy Pros)

- **Customer:** Homeowners with older, under-insulated or leaky attics and ducts, in hot/cold climate metros (Phoenix, Dallas, Salt Lake)
- **Problem:** High energy bills, uncomfortable rooms, dusty or leaky ducts, degraded cellulose insulation
- **Offer:** Free attic inspection with infrared imaging, then remove old insulation, seal the attic, add R49 blown fiberglass, Aeroseal duct sealing, often duct cleaning, sometimes air scrubbers or HVAC units
- **Business model:** Per-job sales of installed packages; revenue minus subcontractor cost (~48-50%), commissions and ads
- **Pricing:** Per-job package ticket, often financed. average ticket USD 10000 per job (average cash value); average ticket including financing fees USD 11000 per job; biggest jobs USD 30000-40000 per job Uses consumer financing; cash value strips out financing fees. Utility rebates (e.g. SRP $400 duct sealing, $600 insulation) help sell.
- **Acquisition:** paid_social: Facebook video ads, often Rex on camera in an attic, pitching a free attic inspection with pain-point hooks; leads cost about $75 each; ad spend kept ~10% of revenue; ad return said to be 8-9x on bad months and 15x on good ones.; other: Commissioned in-home sales reps and project managers use infrared cameras to show leaking air; appointment setters and automations in High Level follow up leads.
- **Delivery:** Almost all work is subcontracted to small local insulation/duct companies who bring their own equipment and often buy materials (~48-50% of ticket goes to sub). Cozy Pros keeps a project manager as homeowner contact (about 60 jobs/month with one PM). Subs found via suppliers, Google, Facebook Marketplace and competing quotes.
- **Unit economics (claims):** Average ticket ~$10k; ~52% gross margin after commissions; Target 20% net, satisfied with 15%; Ad spend ~10%, commissions ~10%, overhead ~10%; About 60 jobs a month in Phoenix with one project manager; Over $1M net profit per metro possible with subcontract model plus Facebook ads
- **Tools:** High Level (Go High Level), Facebook ads, Infrared/thermal cameras, Custom-built CRM, Aeroseal, Consumer financing
- **Startup cost:** Low with subcontracting; in-house route would need insulation machine $15-20k, removal machine ~$10k, duct machine $5-10k
- **Time to first revenue:** not stated
- **UK applicability [E]:** 3/5 — UK homes have different insulation norms (loft insulation, no widespread ducted AC), so Aeroseal duct sealing and Phoenix-style ducts largely do not apply. Loft insulation, ventilation or heat-pump related work is plausible but with different regulation, grants (ECO, etc.) and pricing.
- **Risks:** Subcontractor quality and relationship risk; Sales rep reliance, in-person sales required; Dependence on Facebook lead cost (~$75/lead); Seasonality and weather disruptions (ice storms); Tax credit and rebate changes; Insurance and liability costs
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 4/5 (Paid Facebook ads are fully software-driven (creative, targeting, optimisation by agents); only on-camera video content needs a human occasionally.); onboarding 2/5 (Lead intake and appointment setting can be automated via CRM, but an in-person inspection and sales presentation is needed.); delivery 0/5 (Physical attic work by subcontractors; only subcontractor dispatch and scheduling could be tooled; a project manager is on site.); billing 3/5 (Invoicing and financing links can be automated; commissions and sub payments need routine checks.); support 2/5 (Warranty and quality issues involve site visits and sub management.)

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Free attic inspection as ad hook** (acquisition)
1. Run Facebook video ads with the owner in an attic talking about pain points
2. Offer a free attic inspection as the call to action
3. Appointment setters book leads via High Level automations
4. Inspect with infrared cameras and show homeowners the images
5. Present a tailored package and add on air scrubbers or duct cleaning based on symptoms
> "our ads are really just focused on providing a free attic inspection and once we do that then we kind of know the solution" — Rex Lindsay (~27:18)

**Infrared imaging as sales visual** (sales)
1. Give every sales rep and project manager an infrared camera
2. Turn on the AC or heater on arrival
3. Capture images of ducts and attic leaks
4. Show the glowing leaks so homeowners see money burning
> "show them the picture of like the glowing red and yellow and they're like oh my they just see like their money like burning up." — Chris Koerner (~1:22)

**Find and manage subcontractors** (operations)
1. Ask equipment and material suppliers for recommended subs
2. Search Google, Facebook Marketplace for small local companies
3. Use competing customer quotes to identify cheaper subs
4. Vet several; expect early failures
5. Keep a project manager on site to control homeowner communication
6. Keep subs busy so they depend on you
> "The best way to find really good subs is through the suppliers." — Rex Lindsay (~19:38)

**Self-liquidating cheap front-end offer** (pricing)
1. Advertise a low-price duct cleaning offer that covers lead cost
2. Sell it over the phone
3. Upsell air scrubber, duct sealing or bigger jobs once on site
> "you can come up with this like really good offer on the front end to bank on some of the upsells" — Rex Lindsay (~32:11)

**Stack utility rebates** (sales)
1. Check local utility rebates for duct sealing and insulation
2. Include them in the quote to shorten payback
> "They give $400 just for duct ceiling and then they'll they'll give $600 for insulation." — Rex Lindsay (~16:35)

## Lessons

- *operations*: In home services, the money is in marketing and sales; labor can be rented via subcontractors.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Subcontracting moves equipment, repair and liability risk onto the sub and lowers capital needs.
- *offer_design*: Use a low-barrier front-end offer then upsell to grow ticket size.
- *customer_acquisition*: Visual proof (thermal imaging) makes a data-driven sale to homeowners.
- *niche_selection*: Choose extreme-weather markets for the strongest demand for comfort and efficiency work.
- *offer_design*: Sell the whole outcome (comfort, health, energy) rather than a single product, and sell the main course not an add-on.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Keep ad spend, commissions and overhead each near 10% to hit a 15-20% net target.
- *scaling*: Recurring consumable replacement (air scrubber cells every 2-3 years) gives repeat touchpoints.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Handing jobs to subs without oversight causes problems; keep a project manager and control communication.
- Your first subcontractor probably won't be the good one; vetting takes time and some will need to be dropped.
- Running everything in-house was costly: heavy equipment, ~100 employees, $15k/month rental repairs, higher liability.
- Ice storms and weather can halt work in some markets like Dallas.
