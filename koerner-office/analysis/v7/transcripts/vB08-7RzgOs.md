## Tactics and playbook steps

**Start with a niche you already understand** (niche_selection)
1. Choose an industry where you have worked or run a service business.
2. List the recurring tasks, delays, and frustrations operators face.
3. Talk to people in that industry before choosing a software feature.
4. Build a focused solution for a problem they already recognize.
> "They're starting with what they know, because they know the pain points." — Chris Koerner (~6:25)

**Sell a narrow feature, then expand** (product)
1. Unbundle one useful feature from a larger software product.
2. Use the feature to earn trust with one customer segment.
3. Add related software or CRM features as upsells after customers use the first product.
> "Unbundle one little feature from a bigger piece of software. Sell that hard and then start giving them upsells and more and more software offerings." — Chris Koerner (~5:04)

**Find product gaps in customer reviews** (validation_testing)
1. Search G2 for software in a target niche.
2. Collect customer reviews and ask an AI tool to identify missing features users complain about.
3. Check whether the feature can be built as a GoHighLevel snapshot or add-on.
4. Validate that customers will pay before building a large product.
> "If you don't have to do that, just ask chat JBT, help me build a go-high level snapshot from this." — Chris Koerner (~5:26)

**Partner with an existing niche creator** (customer_acquisition)
1. Find a creator with a focused audience in the target industry.
2. Offer to build software for the creator’s audience.
3. Propose a 50/50 split, as the host suggests.
4. The host recommends contacting them through social DMs or PayPal; in the UK, marketing DMs to individuals and sole traders generally require consent.
> "build this for them, split it with them 50, 50, slide it into their DMs on Twitter, Strava, personal Facebook, Venmo, PayPal, anywhere that isn't crowded" — Chris Koerner (~3:33)

**Study a business before copying its model** (validation_testing)
1. Pick one company to study for a week.
2. Compare its current offer and pricing with older versions of its website in the Wayback Machine.
3. Check current reviews on G2 or Trustpilot for complaints and product gaps.
4. Use the Meta Ads Library and Google search to inspect its ads.
5. Research the founder and look for evidence of how the company operates.
> "pick one and spend the next week studying them, studying their pricing, their website, their ads in the meta ads library, Google that." — Chris Koerner (~18:50)

**Check recurring costs before choosing one-time pricing** (finance_unit_economics)
1. List recurring platform, support, advertising, and labour costs.
2. Compare those costs with the expected lifetime revenue per customer.
3. Use recurring pricing when customers receive ongoing access and support.
4. Do not rely on one-time payments unless acquisition and retention economics cover ongoing obligations.
> "When you copy any one of these businesses, ask yourself, just my pricing cover, my high level bill, plus my time, plus my ads, plus my support every single month, forever." — Chris Koerner (~15:37)

**Localize a proven software model** (product)
1. Look for a language or country where businesses lack local CRM options.
2. Choose a market you can understand and support.
3. Translate snapshots and support documents with AI, then review them for accuracy.
4. Adapt pricing, integrations, and support to the local market.
> "buy a domain in their language, translate the snapshot with Claude, Chechi, Petit, Grok, whatever, build the support docs with Chechi, Petit, Claude, Grok, Gemini, whatever, done." — Chris Koerner (~12:52)

**Build retention into the product ecosystem** (offer_design)
1. Bundle training, software, coaching, and community around one customer workflow.
2. Keep related resources and relationships in the same product environment.
3. Make the product useful beyond the initial training purchase.
> "When your training relationships are inside the software, your peer group, the community, it's all in there." — Chris Koerner (~9:24)

**Use a low-cost trial to validate demand** (validation_testing)
1. Use the platform trial to explore features and build a first version.
2. Try to secure a paying customer before committing to ongoing software costs.
3. The host promotes an affiliate link with a longer trial; the duration is promotional and not a customer result.
> "You can use that to all to get your first paying customer. So you can de-risk paying for software entirely." — Chris Koerner (~19:38)

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Start in an industry you understand so you can identify real recurring problems instead of guessing what customers need.
- *offer_design*: Build a painkiller that solves a problem customers already feel, rather than a feature they merely find interesting.
- *other*: A focused, simple feature can earn trust and create a path to selling a broader product.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Recurring revenue is better suited to an ongoing software product when platform and support costs continue every month.
- *offer_design*: Bundling software with training, community, and coaching can make a product more useful and harder for a customer to replace.
- *validation_testing*: A business can learn from a competitor's reviews, pricing history, and product changes before committing to a build.
- *niche_selection*: Localizing an existing software model can reveal markets where entrepreneurs have fewer familiar options.
- *mindset_execution*: Existing industry experience is useful even if a previous business attempt failed, because it reveals how operators work and what they dislike.
- *other*: Do not assume weak website design means a weak business; the host says some of the businesses work despite modest-looking sites.
- *legal_risk*: A bundled product can be sticky, but customer trust is fragile if pricing promises change.

## Warnings and mistakes

- The host tells viewers not to behave unethically or infringe trademarks when copying business models.
- The host recommends unsolicited DMs and PayPal messages to potential partners. In the UK, unsolicited electronic marketing to individuals and sole traders generally requires consent; cold email rules differ for limited companies.
- Do not assume a one-time payment will cover an ongoing software, support, and acquisition obligation.
- The host says ClickSlow customers complained about poor customer service and allegedly fake reviews, and says the business went bankrupt.
- The host warns that changing an offer customers thought was lifetime pricing to monthly billing can damage trust.
- The host says paid acquisition must be supportable by customer revenue, especially for a one-time sale.
- The host says expensive software and recurring support costs need to be included in pricing.
