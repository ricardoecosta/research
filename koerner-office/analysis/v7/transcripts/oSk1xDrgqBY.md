## Tactics and playbook steps

**Use personal experience to shape the product** (product)
1. Start from an ingredient or routine you know personally.
2. Test combinations with potential customers instead of assuming they want the first version.
3. Look for a product format that fits an existing daily habit.
> "And then when I started testing now with people, they actually don't want just turmeric in their coffee." — Eli (~3:43)

**Negotiate a low-cash manufacturing arrangement** (operations)
1. Find a manufacturer whose existing equipment and certifications cover most of the process.
2. Offer to fund a small equipment addition the manufacturer needs for the new ingredients.
3. Explain how the new product category could help the manufacturer serve other brands.
4. Negotiate payment terms so the founder can sell product before paying the production invoice.
5. Agree a small initial order and understand what growth the manufacturer expects in return.
> "So every time we produce the bag of coffee, we sold that bag of coffee, and then we got invoice a month later from the manufacturer." — Eli (~8:21)

**Collect early reviews before sending paid traffic** (validation_testing)
1. Send product to friends and family as early testers.
2. Have testers place orders through the store using a discount code so purchases can be verified.
3. Ask satisfied testers for reviews without pressuring people who do not like the product.
4. Use the feedback to check the order and manufacturing process before a larger launch.
> "And we basically said, if you like our coffee, please go online and review this, right? That's the way we got reviews early on." — Eli (~16:57)

**Build social proof before scaling ads** (acquisition)
1. Establish a credible social presence and collect creator content before making a large ad commitment.
2. Seed the product with relevant creators and give the brand time to build a visible track record.
3. Only increase paid acquisition after the store no longer looks unknown to first-time visitors.
> "we should have absolutely taken the time to find influencers first, reach out to whatever was longer to get a real brand on that screen before we did it." — Eli (~18:54)

**Approach small, relevant creators with a low-pressure offer** (acquisition)
1. Identify creators in health, wellness and organic food, often with fewer than 10,000 followers.

**Use performance-based creator partnerships where possible** (pricing)
1. Offer affiliate codes or commissions when a large upfront fee is unaffordable.
2. Compare the commission cost with paid acquisition cost before agreeing to a higher rate.
3. Consider a paid creator test only when the creator already knows and likes the product and the spend is manageable.
> "to keep it to keep it low risk, you know, the performance-based model is kind of leaves some upside for them and minimizes risk for you." — Eli (~27:42)

**Test price and bundle options against shipping economics** (pricing)
1. Test different prices and observe both conversion and average cart value.
2. Offer one, two and three-bag options so customers can increase their order while shipping cost per bag falls.
3. Make subscription savings and free shipping clear, and tell customers when the next subscription order will happen.
4. Keep an easy cancellation path to protect customer trust.
> "I probably chain up pricing 30 to 40 times. Just testing things to figure out what works, what sticks, how do you get the average cart value higher?" — Eli (~12:55)

**Track the full value chain and retention** (finance)
1. Track customer acquisition cost, product cost, shipping, gross margin and repeat purchase behavior together.
2. Estimate customer lifetime value using observed subscription tenure, then revise the estimate as data accumulates.
3. Ask customers why they bought again or did not return.
4. Improve the product and customer experience before increasing ad spend.
> "know your numbers and choose a product where the margins will allow you to stay in business." — Eli (~53:27)

## Lessons

- *finance_unit_economics*: Choose a product with enough margin to cover acquisition, production, shipping and customer service, and track those costs together.
- *finance_unit_economics*: A replenishable product can support subscriptions, but repeat buying and customer lifetime value must be measured rather than assumed.
- *customer_acquisition*: Build trust and social proof before scaling paid acquisition, especially when the brand is new and unfamiliar.
- *validation_testing*: Use customer feedback to understand why people buy, repeat, or leave, then adjust the product and offer.
- *customer_acquisition*: Creator fit and audience relevance can matter more than follower count.
- *offer_design*: A clear subscription benefit, transparent order reminders and easy cancellation can encourage repeat buying without damaging trust.
- *hiring_outsourcing*: Outsource work that consumes too much founder time when the cost fits the business, while keeping oversight of the customer experience.
- *finance_unit_economics*: A small first production run and negotiated payment terms can reduce the cash tied up in inventory.
- *mindset_execution*: Move slowly enough to learn when the business is a side project and the founders have limited time or appetite for large spending.
- *operations*: Good fulfillment and customer service matter because acquisition spending is wasted if the product experience fails after the sale.

## Warnings and mistakes

- The founders spent heavily on ads before building social proof, a meaningful social presence or influencer coverage, and Eli said they wasted money.
- The founders initially underestimated long-term customer value, acquisition cost, product cost, shipping and other expenses, leaving the business near break-even.
- A high product price can limit the number of buyers and raise customer expectations; a single shipped bag was described as costing $36.
- Some customers do not subscribe or return, so strong subscription tenure among loyal customers does not guarantee that every acquired customer will pay back acquisition cost.
- The product brewed more slowly than regular coffee and could leave some brewing machines dirty, creating a product and customer experience challenge.
- Retail growth creates inventory and packaging pressure when purchase orders require stock on short notice.
- The manufacturer expected continued growth to justify its support, which can create pressure to increase ad spending or order volume.
- Large creator audiences did not always translate into sales; a smaller, more relevant account reportedly sold more product.
- The guest said some affiliate applicants did little after joining, so accepting every affiliate request may consume time without producing sales.
- The described Instagram direct-message outreach to individual creators would need to comply with UK PECR consent requirements if used in the UK.
- The transcript is a local Whisper transcript with likely recognition errors in several names, numbers and product terms; ambiguous claims should be treated cautiously.
