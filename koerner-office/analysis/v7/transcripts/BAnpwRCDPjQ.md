## Tactics and playbook steps

**Test the idea at events before committing to a store** (validation_testing)
1. Build a mobile version and book events to see whether families will pay.
2. Track sales and learn which event crowds fit the activity.
3. Use visitor questions and event recognition as signals for whether a permanent venue could have demand.
> "What a good way to prove the demand first? Yeah. With $6,000 or 10, 10,000." — Chris Koerner (~29:02)

**Shorten sessions when parents do not want to wait** (pricing)
1. Start by selling timed sessions.
2. Notice whether the session length creates friction for parents waiting nearby.
3. Tim changed from $5 for 10 minutes to $5 for 5 minutes and says the change doubled the business.
> "I convinced my wife that we needed to go to $5 for 5 minutes. She thought we both thought it was too expensive, but it doubled our business." — Tim (~6:42)

**Make the activity visible from the walkway** (product)
1. Design the pit so passers-by can see the machines operating.
2. Tim says his trailer wall was too high and made the activity hard to see.
3. Use clear signage to explain that visitors pay to play rather than buy the machines.
> "Had I made it lower, people just passing by would have seen more of what was going on." — Tim (~4:13)

**Use event vendors and customers to create partnerships** (acquisition)
1. Talk with other vendors and attendees at each event.
2. Explain the activity and answer questions about parties and bookings.
3. Follow up on event conversations; Tim says a local event led to a Choice Hotels tailgate booking.
> "We talked to everybody at every event because we've learned so much." — Tim (~11:26)

**Give visitors something useful to keep** (acquisition)
1. Try branded stickers for children.
2. Give parents branded magnets with the logo and website so they can keep the contact details on a fridge.
3. Track which event handouts get a positive response.
> "We made them a magnet, so you could just put them on your fridge." — Tim (~9:53)

**Automate session timing with a visible countdown** (operations)
1. Record the customer's name and session length in the app.
2. Show the session on a monitor with a short setup buffer.
3. Change the display color and flash it as time runs out so parents can help enforce the end of the session.
> "As it gets closer to the end of the time, it changes colors to warn them and it will start to flash." — Tim (~18:34)

**Build software by testing and revising** (automation_ai)
1. Tim says to be patient and use planning mode to discuss the app before asking the tool to build.
2. Test the result, report issues, and repeat the cycle.
3. Add features as operational needs become clear.
> "This is a lot of my hours just sitting here testing it, telling it to fix this, testing it, telling it to add this." — Tim (~37:00)

**Make the app handle repeatable store operations** (operations)
1. Track sessions, sales, customer accounts, inventory, events, and event requests.
2. Use the same system for gift cards, memberships, and the newsletter.
3. Tim designed it to be reset and configured for a future franchisee.
> "It tracks my inventory, it tracks my financials. I can sell gift cards and memberships. It tracks all of it." — Tim (~36:30)

**Price longer sessions more cheaply per minute** (pricing)
1. Start the store offer at $15 for 15 minutes.
2. Reduce the per-minute price as session length increases.
3. Consider memberships with clear monthly or daily included-session benefits; the transcript does not state membership prices.
> "So, we're going to do 15 minutes for 15 bucks and then as you scale time, it gets significantly cheaper per minute." — Tim (~16:12)

**Use event feedback to improve the offer** (validation_testing)
1. Ask visitors what they expect the business to sell and where they expect to find it.
2. Use repeated questions to shape store offerings and signage.
3. Tim says people repeatedly asked where the store was and whether they could buy the machines.
> "We got two main questions at at all the events this year. Where's your store, and do you sell these things?" — Tim (~10:10)

**Plan for demand variability at outdoor events** (finance_unit_economics)
1. Compare event fees with likely crowd size, not just the event's reputation.
2. Allow for weather changes that can reduce footfall.
3. Tim says fair fees were based on expected attendance, but actual crowds and sales varied.
> "They're very much dependent on the size of the crowd. So, they're charging you based on the numbers of people they saw last year." — Tim (~10:51)

**Get permission before marketing to collected emails** (legal)
1. If collecting customer emails in the UK, explain what the address will be used for.
2. Use consent or another applicable lawful basis before sending marketing, and record the permission where required.

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Use a lower-cost mobile test to learn about demand before committing to a permanent venue.
- *offer_design*: Shorten or reshape an offer when customer behaviour shows that the original format creates friction.
- *customer_acquisition*: Make the value of an unfamiliar activity obvious to people passing by.
- *customer_acquisition*: Use conversations at events to learn and build relationships that can lead to future bookings.
- *automation_ai*: Software can remove repetitive session tracking and help a physical business run more smoothly, but the physical activity still needs people.
- *automation_ai*: Build and test AI-generated software through repeated checks and corrections.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Treat weather and event crowd size as major drivers of pop-up economics.
- *mindset_execution*: A side business can be built alongside employment, but the founder should understand the workload before adding longer opening hours.
- *mindset_execution*: Small businesses do not need to be billion-dollar ideas to be useful; multiple modest businesses can contribute to the same goal.

## Warnings and mistakes

- The trailer walls were too high for passers-by to see the activity; the setup could blend in with other fair trailers.
- Some customers thought the business sold the machines, so the offer was not immediately clear.
- Outdoor event results depend on weather and crowd size, so event fees can be lost when footfall is poor.
- The first event generated little revenue and was effectively a loss.
- The dump trucks Tim bought did not work in the pit as designed.
- Tim says he did not have his store numbers ready when asked about potential capacity and profit; the host's maximum-capacity scenario is hypothetical.
- The plan to retain customer emails for marketing is not accompanied by any description of consent or legal basis. A UK operator should follow PECR and data-protection requirements before marketing to individuals or sole traders.
- Tim describes his belief that the market will grow as a personal view and acknowledges possible builder's delusion.
