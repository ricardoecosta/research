## Summary

Grant LaFontaine, founder of Whatnot (live-shopping marketplace, claimed ~$20B valuation), tells the story of starting it with Logan Head after abandoning a full-service Craigslist idea because delivery economics failed. They chose Funko Pops from eBay sales data, solved the marketplace chicken-and-egg problem by being the first seller, and grew buyers through YouTube influencers and a viral share-to-enter giveaway. Live selling launched in July 2020 and drove growth to roughly $1B in sales within about two years. The key takeaways are to start in a tiny niche, seed one side of a marketplace yourself, be customer-driven rather than vision-driven, and use unscalable growth hacks early. The host also lists ecosystem opportunities around Whatnot: wholesale supply marketplaces, multi-channel networks and tools.

### I1. Whatnot live-shopping marketplace (started as Funko Pop marketplace)

- **Customer:** Collectors and hobby buyers (buyers) and individual or business sellers of collectibles, fashion, food and more (sellers)
- **Problem:** eBay-style marketplaces had not innovated in 20 years and lacked a social, discovery-driven experience; niche sellers were limited to local buyers
- **Offer:** Live video auctions and shopping where sellers reach a national audience and buyers chat and discover items with like-minded people
- **Business model:** Take rate of roughly 10% of gross merchandise value on seller sales
- **Pricing:** Commission on sales. approximate take rate percent 10 of gross sales Funko Pop giveaway prizes of $500 to $1,000 per week were used for buyer acquisition.
- **Acquisition:** affiliates_influencers: Partnered with Funko Pop YouTube influencers; one influencer in the third week of December 2019 drove the first ~30 sales.; organic_social_content: Viral weekly giveaway of a $500-$1,000 Funko Pop; entrants got extra raffle tickets for referral links or codes and shares on Twitter/Instagram. Took over Funko subreddits and Facebook groups.; facebook_groups_communities: Giveaways spread through Funko Pop subreddits and Facebook groups.; marketplace_platform: Cross-listed new sellers' items on other marketplaces using the Whatnot account to get them sales and retain them.; paid_social: Virtually no paid ads early on; now spends an obscene amount on paid ads.
- **Delivery:** Sellers ship goods to buyers (including perishable items like crab by post); Whatnot provides the app, live video, payments and authentication. Early on the founders were the seller, sourcing items from ~100 online stores with a team in Brazil and authenticating them.
- **Unit economics (claims):** Take rate ~10% of GMV; Sept 2020 about $250-300K monthly sales; Year-end 2020 $2.3M total sales; following year about $1B in sales; Top seller does well over $100M a year
- **Tools:** Terapeak, Y Combinator, Custom pricing algorithm built on scraped Funko price data
- **Startup cost:** Founders put in roughly $100-200K of personal savings; raised about $250K from YC.
- **Time to first revenue:** Launched Dec 2019 and made first sale in the first week; about 30 sales in the first month.
- **UK applicability [E]:** 2/5 — Whatnot already operates in the UK, so a new entrant would face a funded incumbent; a solo founder cannot replicate marketplace liquidity or video infrastructure cheaply. Niche marketplace tactics do transfer.
- **Risks:** Marketplace chicken-and-egg problem; Pricing algorithm errors let buyers exploit mispriced items; First giveaway flopped and cost money; Investors saw the Funko niche as too small
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 3/5 (Referral giveaways, influencer partnerships and paid ads could be run largely by software, but influencer outreach needs human relationships.); onboarding 4/5 (Seller onboarding is self-serve in-app.); delivery 1/5 (Physical goods must be shipped by sellers; live selling needs a human on camera.); billing 5/5 (Payments and commission are handled in software.); support 3/5 (Disputes, authentication and trust issues need human review, with heavy tooling.)

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Be the first seller to solve marketplace chicken-and-egg** (acquisition)
1. Solve for one side of the marketplace first
2. Act as the seller and source inventory yourself
3. Authenticate items to add value
4. Build demand before opening to third-party sellers
> "you solve for one side of the equation. And so we were initially the seller on what not." — Grant LaFontaine (~20:27)

**Viral share-to-enter giveaway** (acquisition)
1. Give away a sought-after grail item weekly ($500-$1,000)
2. Give extra raffle tickets for each referral link or code used
3. Allow unlimited sharing on Twitter and Instagram
4. Push-notify past entrants for the next giveaway
5. Persist past a flop: grew from ~100 to ~250-300 entries by the second or third run

**Pricing algorithm using liquidity floor** (pricing)
1. Scrape sites for each item's sales
2. Set a liquidity floor such as 10 sales in 30 days
3. Price using past 10 sales
4. Only list items you can actually secure

**Pick a niche with trend data** (validation)
1. Use Terapeak to compare sales by category on eBay over time
2. Pick the category with the most volume and growth
3. Choose between the top two candidates (Funko and Pokemon)

**Validate unit economics with cold calls before building** (operations)
1. Find delivery managers at companies on LinkedIn
2. Ask how much each delivery leg costs at scale
3. Kill the idea after two calls showed no margin

**Cheap talent for equity** (hiring)
1. Hire a talented but difficult engineer in Brazil cheaply plus equity
2. Pay a designer in equity only

**Cross-list new sellers' items to other marketplaces** (operations)
1. List sellers' items through the platform account on other marketplaces
2. Give sellers sales to retain them

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Start in a tiny niche where you can deliver a great experience; big companies like eBay and PayPal began niche.
- *validation_testing*: Be customer-driven, not vision-driven; be willing to change direction fast when evidence says so.
- *mindset_execution*: Bias to action: you learn by doing, but cheap diligence (a few calls) can kill a bad idea early.
- *customer_acquisition*: Early on use unique unscalable advantages rather than paid ads, which only work later.
- *validation_testing*: Don't give up after one flop; decide whether an experiment has had enough legs and look for compounding on the next run.
- *operations*: Break complex problems down to simple truths; ask 'how do we know that?' until explained in plain terms.
- *scaling*: Selling on a large niche audience platform can be a bigger channel than a local shop; going online widens the buyer pool.
- *niche_selection*: Ecosystem businesses around a fast-growing platform (supply, MCNs, tools) are opportunities, as with Twitch/StreamLabs.
- *mindset_execution*: If you have to talk about moving fast, you're moving too slow.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Hiring executives from big companies or trusting big-company pedigree is often wrong; require people to explain specifics.
- Mispricing in the early pricing algorithm let buyers exploit them, e.g. a $500 item sold for $150.
- A/B tests are unreliable on network/marketplace systems because changes alter both sides' behaviour.
- Founder visions and stories can be misleading; customers decide.
