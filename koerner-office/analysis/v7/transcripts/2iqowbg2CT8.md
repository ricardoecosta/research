## Summary

Chris Koerner explains how to start and run a farmer's market (using his wife's kids' market at a shopping centre as the example), then interviews Mike McCoy of Prep Pizza, who sold DIY pizza dough kits at farmer's markets (about $60k in a first summer, part time) before scaling online via Facebook ads to claimed $3-4M of sales. The video also covers other farmer's market products (baked goods, pickles, fresh pasta) and tips such as sampling, choosing in-city markets, and filming content. Key takeaway: farmer's markets are a cheap, fast way to validate a physical food product, and a market organiser can piggyback on a venue that wants footfall.

### I1. Pizza dough kit (Prep Pizza)

- **Customer:** Home cooks who want better pizza at home, including the outdoor cooking crowd and people who bought equipment and failed.
- **Problem:** Home cooks struggle to get good pizza dough and revert to store-bought; they lack the right flour, ingredients and technique.
- **Offer:** A kit with flour, yeast, salt, honey powder and diastatic malt powder for roughly six pizzas (2 lb package), a two-part poolish recipe, a free video masterclass, chat and phone support.
- **Business model:** One-off product sales of a $30 kit; free masterclass and support drive conversion and satisfaction.
- **Pricing:** Fixed price per kit. farmer's market price USD 30 per kit; online price USD 29.97 per kit Not the cheapest product; customers often say it is expensive, justified by training and support.
- **Acquisition:** other: Farmer's markets 3-4 per week (Wed, Thu, two Saturdays) with a pizza oven, samples of plain dough with minimal toppings; also food shows, golf tournaments and neighbourhood events.; paid_social: Facebook/Instagram ads; hired an agency first with poor results, then took it over himself; about 60 active ads with frequent creative rotation. Also Google ads.
- **Delivery:** Kits assembled in the founders' basement (weighing ingredients, bagging, sealing machine, batches of ~100), later online order fulfilment; training delivered via a free online course; founder answers chat and phone support himself.
- **Unit economics (claims):** About $60k sold in first summer at markets, under 20 hrs/week; Gross margin about 80% on ingredients (agreed to host's estimate); Best month over $160k online sales; Roughly $3-4M online sales over a couple of years; Best farmer's market day close to $3k
- **Tools:** Facebook/Instagram ads, Google ads, Sealing machine, Venmo/QR code payments, Website chat and 800 number
- **Startup cost:** Low; basement production, pre-ordered bags, bulk ingredients, no ad spend at first (amount not stated)
- **Time to first revenue:** Immediate at the first farmer's markets; online was dismal for the first four months.
- **UK applicability [E]:** 3/5 — Concept works in the UK (farmer's markets, Facebook ads), but food business registration with the local council, allergen labelling, labelling rules and UK flour/yeast sourcing apply; imperial/US cottage food laws differ.
- **Risks:** Facebook ad costs consume most margin; Heavy reliance on one ad platform; Manual in-house manufacturing is time-consuming; Food regulation and labelling; Low online conversion at first
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 3/5 (Facebook/Google ads and creative testing can be largely agent-run; the market phase was in-person and would be 0-1.); onboarding 4/5 (Order confirmation and course access are automated by e-commerce tooling.); delivery 1/5 (Physical kit assembly and shipping; needs a 3PL to reduce human work, otherwise manual packing.); billing 5/5 (Online checkout is fully automated.); support 3/5 (Chat and email can be handled by an AI agent with the course content; founder currently answers personally.)

### I2. Run a farmer's market at a shopping centre

- **Customer:** Market vendors (and, indirectly, the shopping centre wanting footfall).
- **Problem:** Shopping centres want to look busy and drive traffic; vendors need venues with existing crowds.
- **Offer:** A ready-made market day on venue land with the venue advertising it, vendors bringing their own tents, tables and chairs.
- **Business model:** Booth fees from vendors.
- **Pricing:** Booth fee per vendor. typical average market USD 50 per booth; typical upper USD 100 per booth Host suggests adult markets could charge more.
- **Acquisition:** partnerships: Called the shopping centre marketing manager; venue offered roped-off parking, electronic billboard, email list and gift cards for judges.; existing_audience: Piggybacks on the shopping centre's existing audience; vendors' families promote on social media. Small Facebook page (300 followers).
- **Delivery:** Mark booth spots with numbered laminated sheets weighed down with sandbags; vendors set up from 8am, market 9am to 12-2pm; organiser runs the day (judging app built by Chris).
- **Unit economics (claims):** 200 booths x $100 = $20,000 per Saturday; Possible $10k profit per weekend, $40k/month; Second market sold 100 booths in about a week
- **Tools:** Custom vibe-coded judging app, Instagram, Facebook page
- **Startup cost:** Almost nothing; the only cost was providing tables and chairs, which they won't repeat.
- **Time to first revenue:** Not stated; first market sold 50 booths over a couple of months.
- **UK applicability [E]:** 3/5 — Works with changes: UK public liability insurance, venue permissions, council street trading rules and food hygiene checks for food vendors.
- **Risks:** Revenue claim is hypothetical, based on a non-profit kids' market; Venue dependence; Weather; Insurance/liability; Requires on-site presence every week
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 2/5 (Venue outreach and vendor recruitment can be assisted by tooling but the venue deal needs human negotiation.); onboarding 4/5 (Vendor sign-up forms, booth maps and instructions can be automated.); delivery 0/5 (Physical event on a Saturday requires people on-site.); billing 5/5 (Booth fees via online payment.); support 3/5 (Vendor questions handled by agent before the event; on-site by humans.)

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Validate food products at farmer's markets** (acquisition)
1. Go to 3-4 markets per week (Wed, Thu, two Saturdays) with 30-40 units
2. Have the spouse cover a second market on Saturdays
3. Give samples of the product exactly as sold, with minimal toppings so the product is tasted
4. Take payment via QR code/Venmo, no team needed
5. Prefer markets inside city limits that are well marketed
> "every single time I'd walk home without and people would buy them" — Mike McCoy (~17:11)

**Move from markets to Facebook ads** (acquisition)
1. Start online once markets prove the product
2. Test an agency, but be ready to take over marketing yourself
3. Run many ad creatives (about 60 active) and rotate them often
4. Use high gross margin to afford ad costs
> "you have to have a way more creatives than you think and you have to rotate them much more often than you think" — Chris Koerner (~27:26)

**Free course and support to increase conversions** (product)
1. Include a free masterclass with every kit
2. Collect troubleshooting issues and reshoot the course
3. Offer live chat and an 800 number answered by the founder

**Pitch a venue that wants footfall** (acquisition)
1. Contact the shopping centre marketing manager
2. Offer a market on parking spaces
3. Ask the venue to advertise on billboard and email list and supply gift cards
4. Mark booths with laminated numbers weighed with sandbags; vendors bring own tables and chairs

**Film content at the market** (acquisition)
1. Set up cameras behind you and let people taste
2. Do not be abrasive about filming; people ignore it

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Farmer's markets are a cheap, fast, real-time feedback channel for physical products before investing in ads.
- *finance_unit_economics*: High gross margin (about 80%) allows aggressive paid advertising, unlike 10-20% margin competitors.
- *customer_acquisition*: Piggyback on existing audiences (a shopping centre's traffic) instead of building your own.
- *offer_design*: Selling a product plus training/support increases success and justifies a premium price.
- *customer_acquisition*: Taking over marketing yourself and learning it produced more in 30 days than four months with an agency.
- *mindset_execution*: Choose businesses you are passionate about when you can afford to.
- *niche_selection*: Selling a product around a hobby is lower-risk than opening a restaurant.
- *scaling*: Some customers only buy at the market and distrust the internet; online conversion is a separate problem.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Don't provide tables and chairs when running a market; only provide ground space.
- Cheap knickknacks and crafts sell poorly at kids' markets; higher-ticket $20-50 items do better.
- Wanting to open a restaurant because friends love your cooking is a trap; running the business is most of the work.
- Selling prepared food at markets requires permits; samples are generally exempt but laws vary by state.
- Not every farmer's market is good; roadside ones outside city limits can have no customers.
- Facebook ad spend takes most of the margin and an agency did not work at first.
