## Tactics and playbook steps

**Test a location with real customers before committing** (validation_testing)
1. Do not assume a busy site will convert; observe whether people have time and reason to stop.
2. Vlad tested a station where people were rushing between transport, then moved two blocks to a station with office-bound foot traffic.
3. Compare sales at each site before investing further in a location.
> "I just moved couple blocks down to the next subway station where people were coming out and going to their offices. And that was like X5 of sales just by switching two blocks." — Vlad (~9:34)

**Build a recurring office route with scarcity** (customer_acquisition)
1. Visit office buildings and speak to reception staff about a repeating schedule.
2. Offer fixed days and one-hour windows across nearby offices.
3. Ask reception staff to share the schedule through the building's internal communications.
4. Use the limited visit window to encourage customers to come while the bike is there.
> "I went to like 10 different offices and I told them, "Hey guys, I'm gonna come to each of you."" — Vlad (~12:34)

**Use social posts to make the mobile schedule predictable** (customer_acquisition)
1. Post locations to social media stories.
2. Post the weekly schedule so existing customers know where to find the bike.
3. Use recurring routes to support repeat visits.
> "post your locations on the stories, post your weekly schedule, etc. This is the best way to build the long-term customer base, returning customer base." — Vlad (~15:16)

**Quote event service against realistic capacity** (pricing)
1. State an hourly fee for the experience.
2. Set expectations at 50 drinks per hour per unit, even if capacity could be higher in some conditions.
3. Charge for booked time whether actual volume is low or high.
4. Add a delivery fee based on distance and transport needs.
> "we can serve 50 cups an hour, maybe 60 depending on the drinks choice. But we keep it at 50 just to be super realistic." — Vlad (~20:45)

**Turn regular customers into event leads** (customer_acquisition)
1. Keep showing up at regular local locations so customers become familiar with the operator.
2. Deliver a positive, distinctive service experience.
3. Stay visible in the local community; a customer may later invite the business to a corporate event or contract.
> "I would never know that one of my regular customers is actually CEO of Vancouver Airport. And then after being my customer for one year, she actually invited us for the big series of events inside the Vancouver Airport." — Vlad (~25:33)

**Validate demand before scaling hardware** (validation_testing)
1. Start operating a unit and observe how customers respond.
2. Use customer support and operational experience to identify product problems.
3. Change the design when the original cart proves too heavy or difficult to set up.

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Test actual customer behaviour before committing to a location; high foot traffic did not help when people were rushing past.
- *customer_acquisition*: A recurring schedule makes a mobile service easier for customers to find and supports repeat custom.
- *niche_selection*: Make the offer visible where likely customers already gather, such as office buildings and events.
- *offer_design*: A differentiated customer experience can help a commoditised product stand out; Vlad emphasised the visible manual drink-making process and positive service.
- *pricing*: Set event expectations using a realistic service capacity and price the booked experience, rather than relying on uncertain drink volume.
- *scaling*: Local operations can generate larger opportunities: repeat customers led to airport events and a retail opportunity.
- *operations*: Make improvements from operating evidence; Vlad kept the coffee-bike concept but redesigned the execution after seeing the first cart's limitations.
- *scaling*: A business can expand beyond the founder's own operating hours by selling a proven setup to other operators and providing optional support.
- *automation_ai*: Use software and AI to reduce repetitive lead work, but the transcript's example still involved many human calls and physical operations.
- *mindset_execution*: Do not let planning become a substitute for testing and execution; accept that early attempts may cost time or money.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Research local food and trading regulations before buying or building a cart. Vlad's first imported cart was not compatible with local requirements and needed substantial rework before approval.
- A busy site is not necessarily a good selling site. People at the first transport location were in too much of a hurry to stop.
- The first cart's weight and layout made it difficult to move and unsafe or impractical to ride uphill or with blocked vision.
- Plan for operational setup time and transport; the original cart required moving equipment from a car each morning.
- Do not promise maximum event throughput without accounting for drink choice and real operating conditions; Vlad used a conservative stated capacity.
- Vlad said he avoids placing a new owner where it could conflict with existing owners, but described this protection as a gentleman agreement rather than a signed agreement.
- The transcript contains likely Whisper transcription errors, including names and phrases such as “go hilevel,” “coldismism,” and “20,000 to store”; treat those details cautiously.
