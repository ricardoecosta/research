## Tactics and playbook steps

**Reverse engineer a digital business from public evidence** (validation_testing)
1. Find businesses on an award list, marketplace or other public directory.
2. Search the company name on Google and review its current website, offer, features and testimonials.
3. Check the Meta Ads Library for active advertising.
4. Use the Wayback Machine to compare the current site with earlier versions and see how pricing and positioning changed.
5. Use public information to form hypotheses about acquisition, customer count and revenue; label estimates as estimates.
> "Because their pricing may have started out as a flat fee, or as $10 a month, or as $1,000 a month. We need to see how they learned" — Chris Koerner (~6:17)

**Reduce friction in a niche software offer** (offer_design)
1. Identify the extra work customers must do after buying software.
2. Add setup, useful data or a ready-made workflow when it removes that work.
3. Sell the outcome and time saved, not only the software features.
4. Make the product easier to start using and harder to leave by providing continuing value.
> "This is them strategically removing friction, which is a first principle. It's what you want to do in any business in any setting." — Chris Koerner (~9:22)

**Partner with an existing creator instead of building an audience first** (acquisition)
1. Find a creator who already serves the intended niche but does not already sell or use the product.
2. Offer to build and operate the software, handle fulfillment and customer service, and share revenue.
3. Use the creator's audience to test demand before investing in audience building.
4. The host suggests trying less crowded social channels for outreach; check applicable consent rules before sending UK marketing DMs.
> "You build this same thing, partner with them, and say, "Hey, I built this awesome SMS automation platform" — Chris Koerner (~10:24)

**Keep a proven price rather than undercutting automatically** (pricing)
1. Review how long the existing price has been used.
2. If the offer and price appear stable, test the value proposition before lowering the price.
3. Avoid assuming that a competitor's $97 monthly price should become $77 with a smaller setup fee.
> "If I were you copying this, I would not change the pricing. This sounds like it's the sweet spot." — Chris Koerner (~8:22)

**Test a charity-backed guarantee** (offer_design)
1. Offer a specific refund or charity donation if a customer does not like the product after a stated trial period.
2. State the amount and timing clearly.
3. The example offers a $100 check or $150 donation after a site has been live for 90 days.
> "If you don't love our software after your site has been live for 90 days, we'll give you a check for $100" — Chris Koerner (~15:34)

**Consider a lifetime price for flat-cost software** (pricing)
1. Compare a recurring subscription with a lifetime offer.
2. A lifetime offer may stand out when the underlying platform fee does not rise with each additional customer.
3. Account for long-term service and support obligations before choosing a one-time price.
> "So, a lifetime price makes sense when you're white-labeling software that's a flat fee, no matter how many sub accounts, aka customers that you have." — Chris Koerner (~19:05)

**Study ad longevity and test new creative** (acquisition)
1. Look at how long a competitor's active ads have been running.
2. Treat a long-running ad as a possible sign that the creative is working, while recognizing that the company may simply not be monitoring it.
3. Test simple static creative against short videos that show a before-and-after outcome.
> "And sometimes simple text, transparent background, stock photo ads work. Usually video ads work even better." — Chris Koerner (~13:50)

**Use specialist research tools and lower-cost access** (validation_testing)
1. Use BuiltWith or Wappalyzer to identify website technology.
2. Use SimilarWeb for traffic estimates and Sensor Tower for app download estimates.
3. Use review sites such as G2 to identify product gaps.
4. Compare the cost of a paid research account with hiring someone who already has access for a small, specific task.
> "Total life hack. If you want to know how much traffic a website gets, SimilarWeb. That is the only one." — Chris Koerner (~22:03) [quote not matched verbatim]

**Use competitor reviews and hiring activity as product research** (product)
1. Collect software reviews and summarize what customers say the product is missing.
2. Review a competitor's job listings for mentions of upcoming products, features or initiatives.
3. Use the findings to choose a product gap to test.
> "G2 is the best for that. Bombora, b o m b o r a, is also really good for stuff like that." — Chris Koerner (~23:53)

**Track changes to competitor websites** (operations)
1. Choose a competitor page or price that matters.
2. Set a website-change monitor to alert you when the page changes.
3. Use the alerts to review new prices, products or availability.
> "Or you want to see if your competitors' pricing changes at any point, you can use something like Distill, Visual Ping, or Change Tower" — Chris Koerner (~24:05)

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Study a business's earlier website and pricing to understand what it learned, rather than copying only its current presentation.
- *offer_design*: Remove work and uncertainty for customers by including setup, data or a ready-to-use system.
- *offer_design*: Sell the result customers want, not just a list of software features.
- *customer_acquisition*: A founder does not need to build an audience before launching; they can partner with a creator who already has one.
- *pricing*: Do not underprice by default. Make sure the offer communicates its value before lowering the price.
- *finance_unit_economics*: A software offer can stand out through a lifetime price when the underlying platform cost does not increase with each extra account.
- *offer_design*: Use specific guarantees to reduce buyer hesitation, and state the period and refund or donation clearly.
- *niche_selection*: A crowded category can still contain room for another business if customers have not found or adopted the available product.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Look beyond an attractive headline price and consider the underlying cost structure and how the offer differs from recurring subscriptions.
- *validation_testing*: Use customer reviews, job listings and competitor page changes to find product gaps and signals about upcoming plans.
- *scaling*: More competitors can increase awareness of a category and grow demand for the original business as well.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Do not infringe on a competitor's trademarks, intellectual property or trade secrets when building an inspired product.
- The host's calculations from customer counts and public prices are estimates, not verified financial statements.
- Do not automatically undercut a competitor's price; the host argues that a lower price can fail to communicate the value.
- A long-running ad could reflect neglect rather than success; the host presents ad longevity as a hypothesis, not proof.
- The host says not to waste the most valuable part of a website's first screen with empty space.
- The video includes a disclaimer that it is educational, not financial advice, and does not promise results.
- The host promotes HighLevel through an affiliate link and invites viewers to join his paid community.
- The host suggests creator DMs as an outreach tactic; UK rules can restrict unsolicited marketing messages to individuals and sole traders.
- Physical rentals and home services need people to transport, install, maintain or arrange physical goods.
