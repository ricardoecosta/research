### I1. Buc-ee's merchandise reseller (Texas Snacks)

- **Customer:** Buc-ee's enthusiasts and Texas expats around the world
- **Problem:** Buc-ee's does not sell online, so fans outside Texas cannot buy its branded products
- **Offer:** Online store (texassnacks.com) shipping Buc-ee's branded shirts, Beaver Nuggets, jerky, candy, plushies and hats
- **Business model:** Retail arbitrage / e-commerce markup on third-party branded goods; one-off product sales.
- **Pricing:** Markup on retail: roughly 1.4x-2x the store price. Halloween shirt, store price ~18 USD USD 33-35 per shirt; Beaver Nuggets, store price 5 USD USD 9 per bag; candy, store price ~4 USD USD 8 per bag Jerky only marked up about 40% because higher prices are hard to justify; Buc-ee's-logo items sell best, non-branded and generic Texas items sell poorly.
- **Acquisition:** organic_social_content: Launched by buying one of everything (~3,000 USD) to get Buc-ee's attention and went massively viral; first month did 160,000 USD.; existing_audience: Email list: after each restock they list items and send an email to customers.
- **Delivery:** Pre-order a large palletised order with the store, collect with a box truck or Sprinter van, unload into own warehouse, list on Shopify, email the list, pick/pack/ship; about a dozen employees.
- **Unit economics (claims):** Spend millions per year at full retail; First month revenue 160,000 USD; Sell thousands of dollars per day; Took a few years to reach profitability; margins not great; About a dozen employees
- **Tools:** Shopify, Email marketing
- **Startup cost:** About 3,000 USD initial inventory (one of everything); later a warehouse and staff
- **Time to first revenue:** First month (160,000 USD revenue)
- **UK applicability [E]:** 3/5 — The model (reselling a cult regional brand to people who cannot reach it) can work in the UK, but Buc-ee's itself is US-only; UK equivalents need a brand with cult demand and no online store. Selling branded goods and food requires trade-mark care and UK food labelling/hygiene compliance.
- **Risks:** Dependent on an unofficial relationship with the brand; the brand can stop it (Buc-ee's asked them to stop selling fudge); Thin margins and many competitors; Brand could launch its own e-commerce; Food freshness and refrigeration constraints; Inventory risk on gimmick items (a ~2,000 USD giant beaver never sold); Trade-mark/IP risk when reselling branded merchandise
- **Automation [E]:** acquisition 3/5 (Email and content to an existing fan base can be automated; viral launch was a one-off stunt.); onboarding 5/5 (Standard Shopify checkout, no onboarding.); delivery 0/5 (Physical buying trips, truck loading, warehousing and shipping need people every time.); billing 5/5 (Shopify payments.); support 3/5 (Order-status and FAQ support can be handled by agents with human escalation for damaged goods.)

## Tactics and playbook steps

**Get a brand's attention with a big visible purchase** (acquisition)
1. Spot a cult brand that does not sell online
2. Spend a meaningful amount (about 3,000 USD) buying one of everything
3. Use the stunt to get the brand's attention and launch content that can go viral
4. Launch a Shopify store immediately to capture demand
> "I walked into Bies. I noticed that that they didn't sell online" — Chris Koerner (~5:02)

**Restock-and-email loop** (operations)
1. Pre-arrange a large order with the store so it is palletised
2. Collect with a box truck or Sprinter van
3. Shelve in the warehouse and list on the website
4. Send an email to the customer list to trigger a sales spike
> "We unload it, put it on shelves, list it for sale on the website, send an email" — Chris Koerner (~4:08)

**Stay on the brand's good side** (legal)
1. Treat the brand as an unofficial partner and pay full retail
2. Comply immediately when corporate asks (e.g. stop fudge sales over freshness)
3. Avoid products whose quality you cannot guarantee
> "Corporate did ask us to stop selling it because they weren't able to guarantee the freshness" — Chris Koerner (~1:55)

**Price by perceived ceiling, not fixed markup** (pricing)
1. Mark up branded apparel and snacks close to 2x
2. Keep markup low (about 40%) on items already expensive, like jerky, where 20 USD feels unjustifiable
3. Drop or deprioritise items customers do not associate with the brand

## Lessons

- *niche_selection*: Look for beloved brands that do not sell online; the gap between in-store demand and online access is a business.
- *other*: Anchor businesses create local economies around them (restaurants, retail, land value); proximity to an anchor is an investable signal.
- *pricing*: Markups must respect the customer's price ceiling; on already-pricey items take a smaller margin to keep volume.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Loss leaders (gas, cheap charging) pull traffic into high-margin retail.
- *legal_risk*: Reselling a third-party brand works best as a friendly unofficial partnership: pay full price and follow their requests.
- *finance_unit_economics*: A viral, high-revenue launch can still be a hard, low-margin business with many copycats.

## Warnings and mistakes

- Non-branded or generic regional goods do not sell; customers want the specific brand.
- Reselling another company's products depends on their tolerance; they can restrict what you sell.
- Gimmick inventory can tie up cash and never sell.
- Viral launch revenue does not mean profit: margins are thin and profitability took years.
