## Tactics and playbook steps

**Lead with a surprising gift in a cold-call pitch** (acquisition)
1. Call a relevant business contact and open with a specific, unexpected gift rather than a generic product pitch.
2. Offer the gift in exchange for a meeting, then explain the underlying service.
3. Track meeting bookings and compare the result with prior outreach.
> "we would like to give you a free cake if you'd like to take a meeting with us." — William Lindholm (~4:34)

**Tie the offer to a budget connected to revenue** (offer_design)
1. Identify whether the buyer's budget is tied to sales or engineering rather than a lower-priority employee benefit.
2. Frame the gift campaign as a way to start more sales conversations.
3. Test the offer with the buyer before building extensive integrations or infrastructure.
> "You always wanna sell your product and get more customers and you always wanna build your product better so you get more customers." — William Lindholm (~9:41)

**Use a memorable campaign to create a PR loop** (customer_acquisition)
1. Make the campaign visually distinctive enough for recipients to photograph or share.
2. Ask clients to track whether recipients post the gift and whether those posts create inquiries.
3. Use resulting media coverage and inbound interest to attract more campaign customers.
> "And that gives you PR and then they might think, oh, this worked on me because I just booked the meeting and posted it on social media." — William Lindholm (~24:11)

**Measure downstream sales outcomes, not only meetings** (validation_testing)
1. Track how many gifts produce meetings.
2. Ask customers to compare close rates for gift-led meetings with their other meetings.
3. Collect enough data to compare campaign costs with close rates and revenue before making strong ROI claims.
> "If I were you, I would like hound my previous customers and say like, do you have any idea on what your close rate was on these, that like the calls that came from these cakes?" — Chris Koerner (~20:55)

**Automate bakery sourcing with human approval on the deal** (operations)
1. Give the agent clear criteria for finding suitable bakeries, including review quality.
2. Have it source bakeries, contact them with a concrete order size and negotiate a discount.
3. Forward acceptable deals to a human for approval, then let the agent continue negotiation or confirm the order.
> "If the discount is good enough, my agent sends that email to my personal email where I just give a thumbs up or a thumbs down on it." — William Lindholm (~35:18)

**Confirm recipient details before sending** (operations)
1. Send a message ahead of delivery to confirm the recipient's address.
2. Send the cake after the address is confirmed.
3. Follow up by email or phone after delivery.
> "We first send the email that says, hey, in two days I'm sending you a cake is this the right address and then we send the cake" — William Lindholm (~14:27)

**Use a QR code to connect the physical gift to a useful audit** (sales)
1. Prepare a short, relevant Loom video such as an SEO audit of the recipient's website.
2. Place a QR code linking to the video on the cake or its packaging.
3. Use the audit as the reason for a follow-up conversation.
> "They're putting like a video, they're recording a loom video, and they're putting a QR code on the cake, and then we're sending that out." — William Lindholm (~29:28)

**Use gifts to retain existing customers** (operations)
1. Identify a customer anniversary or another meaningful moment.
2. Send a gift that exceeds the customer's normal expectations.
3. Look for follow-on conversations, referrals and repeat purchases.
> "So let's say you have some customers that you have for like a year and then you're like, thank you for being my customer for a year, here's a cake." — William Lindholm (~32:32)

**Make founder events unusually memorable** (customer_acquisition)
1. Invite a high-value founder group to a dinner.
2. Add memorable elements such as a live animal or a jazz band.
3. Create an event people are likely to discuss and share afterward.
> "And then we're like, okay, how do we make this something people talk about? We hired or we released a llama." — William Lindholm (~30:30)

## Lessons

- *validation_testing*: Test demand with direct conversations before investing heavily in integrations or a large product build.
- *niche_selection*: A product can retain customers well and still be too low-priority to scale; solve a pain the buyer already prioritizes.
- *offer_design*: Offers connected directly to sales budgets can be easier for buyers to prioritize than benefits with a less direct link to revenue.
- *finance_unit_economics*: Measure the full path from outreach to meeting to closed sale; meeting rates alone do not establish campaign ROI.
- *automation_ai*: Use human approval for important agent decisions while letting agents handle repetitive sourcing and negotiation work.
- *customer_acquisition*: Novel physical gestures can cut through crowded digital channels, but the value depends on whether the gesture leads to a useful business conversation.
- *scaling*: Build a supplier network and operating capability that competitors find tedious to reproduce.
- *customer_acquisition*: A marketing stunt can help both the sender and the customer when recipients share it publicly, creating a feedback loop of attention and inbound interest.
- *validation_testing*: Keep testing new gift formats and collect outcome data before the current novelty becomes commonplace.
- *mindset_execution*: A memorable event or campaign can generate discussion and attention, especially when it has an unusual, visual element.

## Warnings and mistakes

- The birthday service had strong retention claims but was not a large enough priority for many US companies to justify HR-system access; testing many ads did not produce conversions.
- Being covered by news outlets made people aware of the birthday idea but did not create meaningful inbound demand; publicity alone did not validate the pain.
- A cake creates attention and may help secure a meeting, but it does not close the sale; the client's sales team still has to do that work.
- The novelty may fade if physical gifts become overused; William says the business will need data on what gifts and formats work.
- William says the team was struggling to fulfill incoming demand and needed to fundraise and hire to keep up.
- The claimed 35% meeting conversion rate is not accompanied by customer-level close-rate or revenue data.
